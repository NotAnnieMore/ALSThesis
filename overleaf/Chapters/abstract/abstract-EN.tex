\section*{Abstract}
\label{sec:abstract}

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) has a highly variable clinical course, which complicates the prediction of functional decline and survival from information available near diagnosis. Machine learning may help identify patients at greater risk, but its results depend on how outcomes, class imbalance, validation, and decision thresholds are handled.

This dissertation examines these issues through two studies using the PRO-ACT database. Study~I develops a pipeline for classifying rapid functional progression over three- and six-month observation windows from 35 baseline clinical features. Models were trained with subject-level separation, fold-specific target construction, and Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation. In the primary six-month analysis of 1,392 patients, the selected XGBoost model achieved a held-out PR-AUC of 0.456 (95\% CI: [0.364, 0.568]). At the threshold selected from development data, it detected 72 of 84 rapid progressors, corresponding to 85.7\% recall and 35.1\% precision. SHAP analysis identified baseline ALSFRS-R and forced vital capacity as the main contributors to the predictions.

Study~II replicates and extends a published model for identifying patients who die within 24 months of symptom onset. Seven classifiers and ten imbalance strategies were evaluated using 23 features from 1,502 patients. LightGBM with random oversampling produced the best held-out ROC-AUC, 0.923 (95\% CI: [0.868, 0.966]), and detected 30 of 35 short survivors. By contrast, a multi-layer perceptron achieved a similar ROC-AUC of 0.918 but detected only one short survivor at the default threshold. Lowering this threshold improved recall but did not close the gap, showing that strong ranking performance did not guarantee adequate minority-class detection. The replication also confirmed that BalancedBagging improved balanced accuracy for six of the seven classifiers.

The two studies address different prognosis outcomes but reach a common methodological conclusion: performance cannot be judged from a single ranking metric. Outcome construction, patient-level separation, imbalance handling, and threshold-dependent errors materially change the interpretation of a model. The results support reproducible and explainable analysis of ALS prognosis with public data, although external validation is required before any clinical use.
