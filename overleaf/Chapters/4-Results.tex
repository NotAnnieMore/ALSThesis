\subsection{Eligibility by Horizon}
\label{subsec:results_eligibility}
Starting from $N\!=\!5{,}436$ subjects with at least one ALSFRS-R record in the PRO-ACT database, the effective cohort size is determined by the availability of follow-up measurements within each target-horizon window (Table~\ref{tab:eligibility}).

For the \textbf{6-month horizon} ($H\!=\!180 \pm 7$\,d), the final dataset contains $N\!=\!1{,}392$ subjects (25.6\% of the initial pool), partitioned into DEV ($n\!=\!1{,}113$, 80\%) and TEST ($n\!=\!279$, 20\%).
For the \textbf{3-month horizon} ($H\!=\!90 \pm 7$\,d), $N\!=\!2{,}407$ subjects (44.3\%) are eligible, split into DEV ($n\!=\!1{,}925$) and TEST ($n\!=\!482$).
A total of 720 subjects are eligible at both horizons.
The substantial attrition from 5{,}436 to 1{,}392 at 6 months reflects two structural properties of the PRO-ACT database:
(i)~many clinical trials had follow-up periods shorter than 180~days, so subjects were never assessed near the 6-month mark;
and (ii)~ALS patients who deteriorated rapidly may have dropped out, been transferred to palliative care, or died before the 180-day window, creating informative censoring that disproportionately removes fast progressors.
Consequently, the 6-month cohort is likely enriched for slower-progressing subjects relative to the full PRO-ACT population, a caveat that should be borne in mind when interpreting the results.
Nevertheless, the 6-month horizon yields more temporally stable slope estimates (99.8\% based on $\geq 3$ data points, median 7) and a prediction window that is more clinically actionable for treatment planning.

\subsection{Target Definition and Slope Distribution}
\label{subsec:results_target_summary}
For each subject, functional progression is quantified as the ALSFRS-R slope between baseline and the target horizon, expressed in points per 30~days (see Section~\ref{sec:methods_slope}).
Negative slopes indicate functional decline; the more negative, the faster the deterioration.

The distribution of slopes is left-skewed for both horizons:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{6-month:} mean $= -0.95 \pm 0.89$\,pts/30d, median $= -0.80$, range $[-5.23,\; +0.85]$.
  \item \textbf{3-month:} mean $= -0.90 \pm 1.16$\,pts/30d, median $= -0.68$, range $[-7.79,\; +2.16]$.
\end{itemize}
The 3-month slopes exhibit higher variance ($\sigma = 1.16$ vs $0.89$), consistent with fewer data points per slope estimate and wider measurement noise over a shorter observation window.
Despite this, 90.5\% of 3-month slopes are based on $\geq 3$ ALSFRS-R measurements (median $n_{\text{points}} = 4$), and 99.8\% of 6-month slopes use $\geq 3$ points (median $n_{\text{points}} = 7$), confirming adequate estimation quality for both horizons.

The binary target (\textit{rapid} vs \textit{slow}) is derived \textbf{fold-wise} during cross-validation by labelling the worst 30\% slopes in the \emph{training} fold as \textit{rapid}.
For the 6-month horizon, the global 30th percentile corresponds to a slope of $-1.25$\,pts/30d; in practice, the fold-wise cutoffs range from $-1.27$ to $-1.19$ (mean $-1.24$), identifying subjects who are declining by more than approximately one ALSFRS-R point per month.

\subsection{Feature Coverage}
\label{subsec:results_coverage}
Feature availability varies substantially across blocks (Table~\ref{tab:feature_coverage}).
The baseline block has effectively complete coverage: ALSFRS-R total and sex are available for 100\% of subjects, while age is present for 87.1\% ($n\!=\!1{,}212$).
The 180 subjects without recorded age correspond to clinical trials in which age was not collected or was anonymised; because this missingness is administrative rather than outcome-dependent, median imputation is applied without risk of introducing prognostic bias.
Vital signs are available for 80.6\% of subjects at a block level ($n\!=\!1{,}122$), with individual features ranging from 44.9\% (height) to 79.2\% (weight).
FVC data are available for 56.3\% (litres) to 54.5\% (percent of normal), motivating the missingness analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results_fvc_missingness}.
At block level, 60.7\% of subjects ($n\!=\!845$) have at least one non-missing FVC feature.
Study arm is recorded for 91.1\% of subjects. The derived riluzole indicator and concomitant-medication count contain no missing values because subjects without a qualifying pre-baseline record are coded as zero.
Labs are moderately available (creatinine: 64.7\%, ALT: 69.0\%).

\section{Data Quality: FVC Missingness Analysis}
\label{sec:results_fvc_missingness}
% ============================================================

FVC is available for approximately 56\% of the 6-month cohort.
Because missing data can introduce bias if its absence is related to the outcome (Missing Not At Random, MNAR), it is essential to test whether patients with and without FVC differ systematically on key clinical variables.
We compared the two groups across 11 variables using Mann--Whitney~U tests for continuous variables and $\chi^2$ tests for categorical variables, with effect sizes reported as Cohen's~$d$ and Cramér's~$V$ respectively (Table~\ref{tab:fvc_missingness}).

\input{images/tables/step5v2_fvc_missingness.tex}
\break



\noindent\textbf{Key findings.}
Of the 11 tests performed, we first note that the three variables most directly related to the prediction task show no significant association with FVC availability:
ALSFRS-R at baseline ($p\!=\!0.345$, $d\!=\!0.027$), age ($p\!=\!0.459$, $d\!=\!0.031$), and sex ($p\!=\!0.408$, $V\!=\!0.022$).
The target slope approaches borderline significance ($p\!=\!0.050$, $d\!=\!0.098$); however, the effect size is trivial ($d < 0.10$), and the $p$-value does not survive correction for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni-adjusted $\alpha\!=\!0.05/11\!=\!0.0045$).
In substantive terms, mean slopes are $-0.91$ (with FVC) vs $-1.00$ (without FVC)~pts/30d---a difference of $0.09$~pts/30d that is clinically negligible relative to the overall slope standard deviation ($\sigma\!=\!0.89$).
Similarly, lab values (creatinine $p\!=\!0.092$, ALT $p\!=\!0.097$) show no significant difference.

The clearest differences involve study arm ($V\!=\!0.357$, $p < 0.001$) and number of concomitant medications ($d\!=\!0.332$, $p < 0.001$). These associations are compatible with variation in measurement protocols across the pooled trials, although the available variables do not establish the reason for the missing records.
Riluzole shows a nominally significant association ($p\!=\!0.015$, $V\!=\!0.065$), but the effect is negligible and does not survive Bonferroni correction ($p > 0.0045$).
Weight ($d\!=\!0.146$, $p\!=\!0.003$) and BMI ($d\!=\!0.468$, $p < 0.001$) also differ between groups. Their availability may be correlated with FVC collection, but this explanation was not tested directly.

FVC availability shows no clear association with baseline ALSFRS-R, age, sex, or the six-month slope after accounting for effect size and multiple comparisons. Together with the association observed for study arm, this pattern is compatible with missingness related partly to trial-level collection practices. It does not prove that the data are \emph{Missing at Random}, and an \gls{MNAR} component cannot be excluded from the available observations.
Median imputation is therefore retained as a pragmatic preprocessing choice rather than as a consequence of a confirmed missing-data mechanism. The Bonferroni threshold ($\alpha/11\!=\!0.0045$) leaves study arm, BMI, weight, and concomitant-medication count as the clearest observed differences between the groups.

\section{Data Quality: BMI Correction}
\label{sec:results_bmi_fix}
% ============================================================

During exploratory analysis of the vital signs table, we identified a systematic data-quality issue affecting \gls{BMI} computation.
A total of 147 height records in the raw PRO-ACT data were labelled ``Centimeters'' but contained numeric values in the range 14.5--19.3, far below any plausible adult stature.
Multiplied by ten, these values yield 145--193\,cm, which falls within a normal adult range; the most likely explanation is therefore a decimal-point shift during data entry (e.g.\ 173\,cm recorded as 17.3).
Because the feature-extraction function \texttt{to\_cm} passes ``Centimeters''-labelled values through unchanged, these erroneous heights propagated into the baseline feature file.
When used directly in the \gls{BMI} formula ($\text{BMI} = \text{weight\,(kg)} \;/\; [\text{height\,(m)}]^{2}$), a height of 14.5\,cm paired with a typical weight of ${\sim}$95\,kg produces an impossible \gls{BMI} of ${\approx}$4{,}500, confirming the records as data-quality artefacts.

After selecting the measurement closest to $t_0$ per subject, 132 of the original 147 raw records remained in the baseline feature file (\texttt{features\_vitals\_t0.csv}, $N = 4{,}438$).
We corrected the issue by applying plausible adult bounds---Height $\in [120, 220]$\,cm; Weight $\in [30, 200]$\,kg---and setting any value outside these ranges to \texttt{NaN}.
This affected 132 height entries and 31 weight entries; the resulting missing values are handled downstream by median imputation inside each model's preprocessing pipeline (see Section~\ref{sec:results_optuna_v2}).
After correction, \gls{BMI} in the six-month analysis cohort ($N = 1{,}392$) ranges from 15.5 to 46.9, consistent with published \gls{BMI} distributions for \gls{ALS} patients in clinical trials~\cite{Anani2023}.
All datasets (used in tuning, ablation, and all subsequent analyses) were regenerated from the corrected vitals file to ensure that no downstream result is affected by the original erroneous values.

\section{Exploratory Analysis: Treatment vs Disease Progression}
\label{sec:results_eda_treatment}
% ============================================================

Three treatment-related features are available in the dataset: clinical trial arm (\texttt{study\_arm}), pre-baseline riluzole use (\texttt{riluzole\_pre\_t0}), and the number of concomitant medications (\texttt{n\_conmeds\_pre\_t0}).
Their distributions are examined against the six-month \gls{ALSFRS-R} slope and a descriptive rapid-progression label based on the global 30th percentile (418 of 1,392 subjects, 30.0\%). This global label is used only in this exploratory section and not for model validation.

\textbf{Important caveat.}
This analysis is observational and does not estimate treatment effects. PRO-ACT aggregates multiple trials, so ``Active'' denotes exposure to different experimental interventions rather than a single treatment. Riluzole use and concomitant medication burden may also be associated with patient characteristics, trial procedures, and recording practices. The comparisons below are therefore descriptive.

\label{subsec:results_eda_arm}

Median slopes are similar: $-0.791$ (Active) vs $-0.819$ (Placebo) points/30\,d.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:results_eda_treat_summary}

Neither study arm nor documented riluzole use is significantly associated with the descriptive rapid-progression label at $\alpha=0.05$. Their continuous slope distributions also overlap. \section{Unified Optuna Hyperparameter Tuning (6-Month Horizon)}
\label{sec:results_optuna_v2}
% ============================================================

Seven model families were tuned with Optuna (60 trials per configuration, \gls{TPE} sampler, seed~42) using subject-wise GroupKFold ($K\!=\!5$), fold-wise binary label definition, and the \gls{DEV} partition only ($n\!=\!1{,}113$).
The seven families---Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, \gls{KNN}, \gls{SVM}, Random Forest, \gls{LGBM}, and \gls{XGB}---were chosen to span linear, distance-based, kernel-based, single-tree, and ensemble paradigms, allowing a systematic comparison across model complexity levels.
Each model was evaluated in both \textbf{unbalanced} and \textbf{balanced} modes---except \gls{KNN}, which lacks native class-weight support, yielding 13 configurations in total.
The objective metric was \gls{PR-AUC} (average precision). After each search, \gls{ROC-AUC} and threshold-dependent metrics ($F_1$, $F_2$ at threshold~0.5) were collected for the selected configuration.
Sixty trials were used as a common computational budget rather than as a formal convergence criterion. All 780 trials completed successfully. Nine of the 13 searches found their final best value between trials 41 and 60, including both XGBoost modes, so the histories do not support truncating the budget at 40 trials. The searched spaces ranged from two parameters for Logistic Regression to nine for LightGBM and XGBoost, although the sampled LightGBM \texttt{subsample} parameter was inactive under the default \texttt{subsample\_freq=0}.
The full tuning required approximately 34 minutes of recorded optimisation time on a single machine, with per-configuration times ranging from 8\,s (Decision Tree) to 768\,s (\gls{SVM} unbalanced).

Table~\ref{tab:step5v2_tuning} and Figure~\ref{fig:prauc_barchart} summarise the results.

\noindent\textbf{Key observations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Top-ranked model.} XGBoost (unbalanced) achieves the highest PR-AUC ($0.434 \pm 0.049$), closely followed by Random Forest (unbalanced, $0.431 \pm 0.042$) and SVM (balanced, $0.425 \pm 0.049$).
    The small differences among the leading configurations show that several model families achieve similar internal validation performance; they do not, by themselves, establish a performance ceiling.
  \item \textbf{Effect of class balancing on PR-AUC.} Weighting produces small changes for Random Forest ($0.431 \to 0.425$), LightGBM ($0.415 \to 0.418$), and XGBoost ($0.434 \to 0.424$). The largest change occurs for SVM, whose PR-AUC increases from $0.389$ to $0.425$. The effect is therefore model-dependent rather than uniformly beneficial or detrimental.
  \item \textbf{Threshold-dependent metrics.} At the default threshold of 0.5, $F_1$ and $F_2$ are low for most unbalanced models, although Decision Tree is a clear exception. Weighting substantially increases these scores for Decision Tree, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, LightGBM, and XGBoost; for example, Logistic Regression $F_2$ increases from $0.028$ to $0.527$. Balanced SVM remains near zero at this cutoff despite its improved PR-AUC. These results show that performance at a fixed threshold depends on the probability scale as well as on ranking ability (Section~\ref{sec:results_threshold_xgb}).
  \item \textbf{Interpretable baselines.} Logistic Regression balanced ($\text{PR-AUC} = 0.407$) performs only $0.027$ below XGBoost unbalanced, making it a competitive interpretable alternative.
    Its coefficients can be directly analysed to understand feature directionality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Balanced vs Unbalanced: Paired Comparison}
\label{subsec:results_bal_vs_unbal}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Figure~\ref{fig:bal_vs_unbal} presents a paired (dumbbell) comparison of PR-AUC and ROC-AUC for each model family across the two class-weighting modes.
The differences between modes are below 0.01 PR-AUC for Random Forest, LightGBM, and XGBoost. Decision Tree improves by 0.016 with weighting, while Logistic Regression changes by 0.006. SVM shows the largest difference, increasing by 0.036 in balanced mode. Thus, class weighting has little effect on ranking performance for most model families in this experiment, but the SVM result shows that this cannot be assumed for every classifier.

The much larger differences in $F_1$ and $F_2$ at threshold 0.5 reflect changes in the scale and distribution of predicted probabilities. They should not be interpreted as equivalent changes in threshold-free discrimination. This distinction motivates selecting the model by PR-AUC and choosing its operating threshold separately on development data.

\subsection{Metric Rank Correlation}
\label{subsec:results_metric_correlation}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We computed Spearman rank correlations between all pairs of cross-validated evaluation metrics across the 13 model $\times$ mode configurations to compare the resulting model rankings.
If two metrics produce highly correlated model rankings, they provide redundant information; if uncorrelated, they capture different aspects of model behaviour.

The correlations quantify agreement between rankings but do not make the metrics interchangeable, since each captures a different aspect of performance. Two patterns are visible:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Threshold-free metrics:} PR-AUC and ROC-AUC are strongly correlated ($\rho\!=\!0.758$, $p\!=\!0.003$), indicating that models which rank probability predictions well under one curve generally rank well under the other.
    The two measures therefore produce broadly similar, although not identical, model rankings.
  \item \textbf{Threshold-dependent metrics at fixed cutoff:} $F_1$ and $F_2$ at threshold~0.5 are almost perfectly correlated with each other ($\rho\!=\!0.984$, $p < 0.001$), while neither shows evidence of a monotonic association with PR-AUC ($|\rho| < 0.08$, $p > 0.8$).
    In these configurations, the fixed-threshold metrics are strongly influenced by whether predicted probabilities cross 0.5 and do not reproduce the ranking obtained from the full probability curves.
\end{itemize}

These findings support the use of PR-AUC as the primary model-selection metric in this imbalanced classification setting. $F_1$ and $F_2$ become informative for the intended operating point only after the threshold has been selected on development data (Section~\ref{sec:results_threshold_xgb}).
Figure~\ref{fig:metric_heatmap} visualises the cross-validation metric values for all 13 configurations, sorted by PR-AUC; the disconnection between threshold-free and threshold-dependent metrics is clearly visible.

\section{Feature Block Ablation}
\label{sec:results_ablation_v2}
% ============================================================

The feature-block analysis applies the hyperparameters tuned on the full feature set to each reduced configuration. It therefore describes the sensitivity of each fitted model family to the available feature blocks without re-optimising the model for every subset.

Four selected model configurations were evaluated across six feature configurations:
\textbf{A}~(core baseline variables, $n\!=\!15$),
\textbf{B}~(A + vitals, $n\!=\!27$),
\textbf{C}~(A + FVC, $n\!=\!18$),
\textbf{D}~(A + vitals + FVC, $n\!=\!30$),
\textbf{E}~(D + treatment, $n\!=\!33$),
\textbf{F}~(full set with labs, $n\!=\!35$).
Blocks B and C are parallel extensions of A rather than consecutive steps. D combines both blocks, while E and F then add treatment and laboratory variables sequentially.

\noindent\textbf{Key observations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The core baseline block contains most of the observed performance.}
    PR-AUC with configuration A ranges from 0.395 for Logistic Regression to 0.434 for LightGBM. Across models, the best configuration improves on A by between 0.000 and 0.025, indicating that the additional blocks produce relatively small changes compared with the information already present at baseline.
  \item \textbf{The effect of FVC is model-dependent.}
    Adding FVC without vital signs (A~$\to$~C) increases PR-AUC from 0.409 to 0.434 for Random Forest and from 0.420 to 0.430 for XGBoost. The corresponding changes are smaller for Logistic Regression ($+0.005$) and slightly negative for LightGBM ($-0.002$). FVC therefore provides the clearest gain for the two unbalanced tree ensembles in this comparison, consistent with its established prognostic relevance in ALS~\cite{Papaiz2022}.
  \item \textbf{Vital signs do not produce a consistent improvement.}
    From A to B, PR-AUC is essentially unchanged for XGBoost and Random Forest, increases by 0.010 for Logistic Regression, and decreases by 0.014 for LightGBM. Combining vital signs with FVC in D reduces the fold-to-fold standard deviation relative to C for all four models, but does not consistently increase the mean.
  \item \textbf{Treatment and laboratory blocks have small and variable effects.}
    Treatment variables change PR-AUC by between $-0.009$ and $+0.001$ from D to E. Adding the two laboratory variables from E to F increases PR-AUC by 0.011 for XGBoost and 0.014 for Random Forest, is nearly neutral for Logistic Regression, and decreases it by 0.007 for LightGBM. From D to F, the net changes are $+0.002$, $+0.013$, $+0.001$, and $-0.006$, respectively.
  \item \textbf{No feature configuration is best for every model.}
    The full set F gives the highest mean PR-AUC for XGBoost (0.434) and Logistic Regression (0.407). Random Forest reaches its maximum with A + FVC (C: 0.434), while LightGBM performs best with the core block alone (A: 0.434). These differences are small relative to the fold standard deviations and were not subjected to separate significance tests.
\end{enumerate}

Overall, the analysis suggests that the core baseline variables account for most of the attainable discrimination in these four configurations. Additional blocks alter performance modestly and differently across model families; among them, FVC shows the clearest improvement for Random Forest and XGBoost.

\section{Threshold Optimisation}
\label{sec:results_threshold_xgb}
% ============================================================

The default threshold of $t\!=\!0.50$ produces very few positive predictions for the selected unbalanced XGBoost model. We therefore compare alternative thresholds using out-of-fold (OOF) predictions from five-fold GroupKFold on the DEV set. Labels for this analysis use the fixed DEV 30th-percentile slope cutoff ($-1.243$ points per 30 days).

Three optimality criteria are evaluated:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{$F_2$-optimal} ($t\!=\!0.05$): $F_2=0.682$. At this threshold all 1,113 DEV participants are classified as rapid, giving recall 1.00 and precision 0.300. The optimum is therefore driven by the strong weighting of recall and provides no separation into predicted classes.
  \item \textbf{$F_1$-optimal} ($t\!=\!0.21$): $F_1=0.479$, with recall 0.874 and precision 0.330. This threshold classifies 79.6\% of DEV as rapid.
  \item \textbf{Youden's~$J$} ($t\!=\!0.35$): $J=0.171$, with recall 0.410 and precision 0.424. This criterion gives equal importance to sensitivity and specificity.
\end{enumerate}

$t=0.21$ is retained as the primary operating point because it maximises $F_1$ without collapsing to an all-positive classification. It nevertheless represents an aggressive classification rule: almost four in five development participants are flagged, and precision is only three percentage points above the rapid-progression prevalence. The threshold is therefore used to evaluate a sensitivity-oriented prototype, not presented as a clinically established cutoff.
Figure~\ref{fig:step6_threshold_sweep} illustrates the multi-metric threshold sweep.

  The $F_1$-optimal threshold ($t\!=\!0.21$, blue dotted) and Youden's~$J$ operating point ($t\!=\!0.35$, purple dotted) are annotated.}

\section{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{sec:results_summary}
% ============================================================

The results obtained so far establish the feasibility and performance baseline for the explainable ALS progression classification pipeline.
The main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cohort viability.} Leakage-free, subject-wise evaluation is feasible using PRO-ACT baseline features and future ALSFRS-R slopes.
    The 6-month cohort ($N\!=\!1{,}392$) yields slope estimates of high quality (99.8\% based on $\geq 3$ data points).
  \item \textbf{Data quality.} A systematic BMI data-quality issue (147 mis-labelled height records) was identified and corrected.
    Associations with FVC availability were compatible with a contribution from trial-level collection practices, although the missing-data mechanism could not be confirmed and an MNAR component remains possible.
  \item \textbf{Treatment features.} Neither study arm nor documented riluzole use is significantly associated with the descriptive rapid-progression label.
    Active and Placebo rates are 26.5\% and 31.6\%, respectively ($p=0.057$), while riluzole groups show similar label rates ($p=0.416$) and slope distributions ($p=0.534$).
    These variables remain candidate predictors of patient and trial heterogeneity, without a causal interpretation.
  \item \textbf{Model selection.} Unified Optuna tuning of 7 model families across 13 configurations identifies XGBoost (unbalanced) as the top-ranked model by PR-AUC ($0.434 \pm 0.049$), followed by RF~unbalanced ($0.431$) and SVM~balanced ($0.425$).
    Several model families obtain similar internal validation performance, with less than 0.01 PR-AUC separating the two leading configurations.
  \item \textbf{Class balancing.} Balanced and unbalanced modes produce similar PR-AUC values for most model families, although SVM improves by 0.036 with weighting. The larger differences in $F_1$ and $F_2$ at $t\!=\!0.5$ mainly reflect shifts in predicted probabilities and reinforce the need to separate model ranking from operating-threshold selection.
  \item \textbf{Feature-block analysis.} The core baseline block retains most of the cross-validated performance. Adding FVC produces the clearest gains for Random Forest ($+0.025$) and XGBoost ($+0.009$), while the effects of vital signs, treatment variables, and laboratory measurements are smaller and vary by model.
  \item \textbf{Metric consistency.} PR-AUC and ROC-AUC produce strongly correlated model rankings ($\rho\!=\!0.758$), supporting the use of PR-AUC as the primary metric.
    Fixed-cutoff $F_1$ and $F_2$ do not reproduce this ranking and are reserved for operating-point assessment.
  \item \textbf{Cross-horizon robustness.} The 3-month horizon ($N\!=\!2{,}407$) produces comparable results (top PR-AUC $0.426$, $\Delta \leq 0.021$ vs 6-month), with the same model ranking.
    The 6-month horizon is retained as the primary analysis due to higher slope quality and greater clinical actionability.
  \item \textbf{Held-out generalisation.}
    Test-set PR-AUC (RF\,$=\!0.459$, XGB\,$=\!0.456$) is slightly above the corresponding cross-validated development means, with no marked loss of discrimination on the held-out partition.
    Random Forest and XGBoost remain closely matched, but the TEST results are not used to repeat model selection.
  \item \textbf{Threshold optimisation.}
    The $F_1$-optimal threshold ($t\!=\!0.21$) achieves recall\,$=\!85.7$\% on TEST, but classifies 73.5\% of participants as rapid and produces 133 false positives ($F_1\!=\!0.498$, $F_2\!=\!0.665$).
    XGBoost achieves the lowest Brier score ($0.197$) among the compared models, with some deviation from ideal calibration at higher predicted probabilities.
  \item \textbf{Explainability.}
    SHAP, LIME, and LR coefficient analysis converge on the same clinical drivers: baseline ALSFRS-R total score is the dominant predictor, followed by respiratory markers (FVC, respiratory rate) and vital signs.
    SHAP--LIME concordance of 40--50\% across case studies, combined with LR coefficient alignment, confirms that the identified features are robust across fundamentally different attribution methods.
    Local explanations reveal that false negatives typically present with high baseline function, highlighting a structural limitation of baseline-only models in detecting rapid decliners with initially preserved function.
  \item \textbf{Error profiling and subgroup equity.}
    Systematic error analysis confirms that false negatives concentrate in patients with mild baseline function (ALSFRS-R $>$42, recall$\,=\,$54.5\%), while the model maintains recall $>$77\% across all other subgroups.
    Performance is balanced between sexes (PR-AUC 0.45 vs 0.47).
    ALSFRS-R severity is the strongest moderator of model performance, with PR-AUC ranging from 0.558 (severe) to 0.206 (mild).
  \item \textbf{Clinical utility.}
    Decision Curve Analysis confirms that the model provides positive net benefit over treat-all and treat-none strategies for thresholds $t \in [0.13,\, 0.57]$, a wide range spanning diverse clinical cost preferences.
  \item \textbf{Data sufficiency.}
    Learning curves show that PR-AUC plateaus at approximately 60\% of the training data, confirming a feature-driven performance ceiling.
    Additional baseline data alone is unlikely to materially improve discrimination.
  \item \textbf{Statistical stability.}
    Bootstrap confidence intervals ($B\!=\!2{,}000$) confirm that all TEST-set metrics have narrow 95\% CIs (width $\leq 0.204$).
    Recall is bounded within $[0.781,\, 0.929]$, and Brier score within $[0.174,\, 0.221]$, indicating that performance estimates are stable given the sample size.
  \item \textbf{Feature effect shapes.}
    Partial Dependence Plots reveal a strong monotonically decreasing relationship between ALSFRS-R total and predicted rapid-progression probability, confirming that the model captures the expected clinical direction.
    FVC shows a non-linear protective effect, while respiratory rate exhibits a more complex pattern suggesting interaction effects.
  \item \textbf{Patient risk report.}
    A proof-of-concept report generator translates model predictions into structured, actionable clinical summaries comprising risk tier, SHAP-based feature attribution, and rule-based monitoring suggestions.
    Reports for 12~real and 10~synthetic patients produce clinically plausible outputs, with ALSFRS-R total consistently emerging as the dominant predictor.
\end{enumerate}

\bigskip
\noindent
Taken together, these results establish Study~I as the original methodological contribution of this dissertation: a leakage-free, explainable, and clinically motivated pipeline for short-term functional progression prediction in \gls{ALS}.
The methodological lessons --- metric alignment, threshold-aware evaluation, and the primacy of baseline functional status --- inform the design of Study~II, described in Chapter~\ref{ch:study2_methods}.
