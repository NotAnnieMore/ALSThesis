\subsection{Eligibility by Horizon}
\label{subsec:results_eligibility}
Starting from $N\!=\!5{,}436$ subjects with at least one ALSFRS-R record in the PRO-ACT database, the effective cohort size is determined by the availability of follow-up measurements within each target-horizon window (Table~\ref{tab:eligibility}).

For the \textbf{6-month horizon} ($H\!=\!180 \pm 7$\,d), the final dataset contains $N\!=\!1{,}392$ subjects (25.6\% of the initial pool), partitioned into DEV ($n\!=\!1{,}113$, 80\%) and TEST ($n\!=\!279$, 20\%).
For the \textbf{3-month horizon} ($H\!=\!90 \pm 7$\,d), $N\!=\!2{,}407$ subjects (44.3\%) are eligible, split into DEV ($n\!=\!1{,}925$) and TEST ($n\!=\!482$).
A total of 720 subjects are eligible at both horizons.
The substantial attrition from 5{,}436 to 1{,}392 at 6 months reflects the requirement for follow-up ALSFRS-R measurements near the 180-day mark, confirming that longer horizons reduce the available sample but also yield more temporally stable slope estimates.

\subsection{Target Definition and Slope Distribution}
\label{subsec:results_target_summary}
For each subject, functional progression is quantified as the ALSFRS-R slope between baseline and the target horizon, expressed in points per 30~days (see Section~\ref{sec:methods_slope}).
Negative slopes indicate functional decline; the more negative, the faster the deterioration.

The distribution of slopes is left-skewed for both horizons:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{6-month:} mean $= -0.95 \pm 0.89$\,pts/30d, median $= -0.80$, range $[-5.23,\; +0.85]$.
  \item \textbf{3-month:} mean $= -0.90 \pm 1.16$\,pts/30d, median $= -0.68$, range $[-7.79,\; +2.16]$.
\end{itemize}
The 3-month slopes exhibit higher variance ($\sigma = 1.16$ vs $0.89$), consistent with fewer data points per slope estimate and wider measurement noise over a shorter observation window.
Despite this, 90.5\% of 3-month slopes are based on $\geq 3$ ALSFRS-R measurements (median $n_{\text{points}} = 4$), and 99.8\% of 6-month slopes use $\geq 3$ points (median $n_{\text{points}} = 7$), confirming adequate estimation quality for both horizons.

The binary target (\textit{rapid} vs \textit{slow}) is derived \textbf{fold-wise} during cross-validation by labelling the worst 30\% slopes in the \emph{training} fold as \textit{rapid}.
For the 6-month horizon, this corresponds to a slope cutoff of approximately $-1.4$\,pts/30d (30th percentile), identifying subjects who are declining at more than one ALSFRS-R point per month.

\section{Data Quality: FVC Missingness Analysis}
\label{sec:results_fvc_missingness}
% ============================================================

FVC is available for approximately 56\% of the 6-month cohort.
Because missing data can introduce bias if its absence is related to the outcome (Missing Not At Random, MNAR), it is essential to test whether patients with and without FVC differ systematically on key clinical variables.
We compared the two groups across 11 variables using Mann--Whitney~U tests for continuous variables and $\chi^2$ tests for categorical variables, with effect sizes reported as Cohen's~$d$ and Cramér's~$V$ respectively (Table~\ref{tab:fvc_missingness}).

\input{images/tables/step5v2_fvc_missingness.tex}

\noindent\textbf{Key findings.}
The three variables most directly related to the prediction task---ALSFRS-R at baseline ($p\!=\!0.345$, $d\!=\!0.027$), age ($p\!=\!0.459$, $d\!=\!0.031$), and the target slope ($p\!=\!0.188$, $d\!=\!0.061$)---show \emph{no significant difference} between patients with and without FVC data.
Similarly, lab values (creatinine $p\!=\!0.092$, ALT $p\!=\!0.097$) and sex ($p\!=\!0.408$) are not significantly associated with FVC availability.

The variables that \emph{do} differ significantly are related to study design rather than disease severity: study arm ($V\!=\!0.357$, $p < 0.001$) and number of concomitant medications ($d\!=\!0.332$, $p < 0.001$) reflect the fact that certain trials systematically collected FVC while others did not.
BMI also differs ($d\!=\!0.468$), which is expected because FVC testing often occurs in the same visit as vital sign measurement.

Since the outcome (slope) and the most clinically relevant baselines (ALSFRS-R, age) are unrelated to FVC availability, the missingness mechanism is classified as \emph{Missing at Random} (MAR), driven by study-protocol factors rather than by the patient's disease trajectory.
This supports the use of median imputation for FVC features without introducing outcome-dependent bias.

\section{Data Quality: BMI Correction}
\label{sec:results_bmi_fix}
% ============================================================

During exploratory analysis of the vital signs table, we identified a systematic data-quality issue affecting BMI computation.
A total of 147 height records were labelled ``Centimeters'' in the source data but contained values below 120\,cm (range 55--72), strongly suggesting that the original measurements were recorded in inches with an incorrect unit label.
When used directly in the BMI formula ($\text{BMI} = \text{weight} / \text{height}^2$), these mis-labelled heights produced physiologically impossible values up to 4{,}512.

We corrected this by applying plausible adult bounds (Height $\in [120, 220]$\,cm; Weight $\in [30, 200]$\,kg), setting any value outside these ranges to \texttt{NaN} for subsequent imputation.
This affected 132 height records and 31 weight records.
After correction, BMI across the cohort ranges from 14.4 to 46.9, consistent with published BMI distributions for ALS patients in clinical trials~\cite{Anani2023}.
All v2 datasets (used in tuning, ablation, and all subsequent analyses) were regenerated with the corrected vitals to ensure that no downstream result is affected by the erroneous values.

\section{Exploratory Analysis: Treatment vs Disease Progression}
\label{sec:results_eda_treatment}
% ============================================================

Three treatment-related features are available in the v2 dataset: clinical trial arm (\texttt{study\_arm}), pre-baseline riluzole use (\texttt{riluzole\_pre\_t0}), and the number of concomitant medications (\texttt{n\_conmeds\_pre\_t0}).
Before incorporating these features into the predictive pipeline, we investigate their descriptive association with the ALSFRS-R slope and rapid-progression rate.

\textbf{Important caveat.}
This analysis is strictly \emph{descriptive} (observational).
PRO-ACT aggregates data from multiple clinical trials, so ``Active'' denotes the experimental arm of different studies (lithium, dexpramipexole, ceftriaxone, among others), most of which did not demonstrate efficacy.
Riluzole use is not randomised and is subject to indication bias.
No causal claims can be drawn without dedicated causal-inference methods (e.g.\ propensity score matching, IPTW).

\subsection{Study Arm: Active vs Placebo}
\label{subsec:results_eda_arm}

Of the 1{,}392 subjects in the 6-month cohort, 506 belong to the Active arm, 762 to Placebo, and 124 have no arm information.
The Active group shows a lower rapid-progression rate ($26.5$\%) compared to Placebo ($31.6$\%), with the NaN group at $34.7$\% (Table~\ref{tab:eda_treat_arm}).
Median slopes are similar: $-0.791$ (Active) vs $-0.819$ (Placebo) points/30\,d.

A $\chi^2$ test of independence between study arm and rapid-progression status yields $\chi^2 = 3.62$, $p = 0.057$ (marginally non-significant at $\alpha = 0.05$).
A Mann-Whitney~$U$ test comparing the slope distributions of Active vs Placebo gives $U = 200{,}472$, $p = 0.229$, confirming no statistically significant difference.
The 5-percentage-point gap in rapid rates is therefore likely attributable to the heterogeneity of the ``Active'' arm and chance variation.

\subsection{Riluzole}
\label{subsec:results_eda_riluzole}

Riluzole---the only disease-modifying therapy approved for ALS at the time of the PRO-ACT trials---was used pre-baseline by 902 subjects (64.8\%).
Patients with and without riluzole show nearly identical rapid-progression rates ($30.8$\% vs $28.6$\%), consistent with the established literature reporting a modest survival benefit but no significant impact on ALSFRS-R decline~\cite{Miller2012}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/figures/eda_treat_riluzole_slope_dist.png}
  \caption{Slope distribution (left) and rapid-progression rate (right) by riluzole use. Median slopes are annotated on each bar.}
  \label{fig:eda_riluzole}
\end{figure}

A $\chi^2$ test confirms no significant association ($\chi^2 = 0.66$, $p = 0.416$).

\subsection{Interaction: Study Arm $\times$ Riluzole}
\label{subsec:results_eda_interaction}

Crossing both treatment variables reveals a noteworthy pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:eda_interaction}): the Placebo + riluzole subgroup has the highest rapid-progression rate ($33.5$\%, $n\!=\!526$), while Active + riluzole has the lowest ($25.7$\%, $n\!=\!315$).
In contrast, both ``no riluzole'' subgroups cluster near $27.5$\%.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{images/figures/eda_treat_interaction.png}
  \caption{Rapid-progression rate by study arm $\times$ riluzole subgroup. The dashed line indicates the global prevalence (30\%).}
  \label{fig:eda_interaction}
\end{figure}

This pattern may reflect confounders: riluzole-taking patients in the Placebo arm may have been prescribed riluzole precisely because of perceived faster decline (indication bias), whereas in the Active arm, the experimental treatment may partially offset progression in the same at-risk subgroup.
Without causal-inference methods, this remains a hypothesis.

\subsection{Concomitant Medications and Top Drugs}
\label{subsec:results_eda_conmeds}

The number of pre-baseline concomitant medications (\texttt{n\_conmeds\_pre\_t0}) shows a non-linear relationship with rapid-progression rate: patients with 1--2 medications have the lowest rate ($22.8$\%, $n\!=\!149$), while those with 6--9 medications have the highest ($34.2$\%, $n\!=\!310$).
This may reflect disease severity or comorbidity burden rather than a direct pharmacological effect.

We further analysed the 15 most frequently prescribed pre-baseline medications by linking back to the raw \texttt{PROACT\_CONMEDS} table (Figure~\ref{fig:eda_top_meds}).
Riluzole is the most common (48.2\% of the cohort), followed by Vitamin~E (25.4\%) and Ascorbic Acid (22.1\%).
Two supplements---Betacarotene ($25.4$\%) and Nutrasense Createam ($26.9$\%)---are associated with below-average rapid rates, while Colecalciferol ($37.3$\%) and Sertraline ($35.3$\%) show the highest rates, although subgroup sizes are small ($n < 80$) and no corrections for multiple testing are applied.

  \label{fig:eda_top_meds}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:results_eda_treat_summary}

None of the treatment variables show a statistically significant association with rapid progression at the conventional $\alpha = 0.05$ level.
The Active arm exhibits a marginally lower rapid rate ($p = 0.057$), but this is confounded by the heterogeneity of the experimental arms across trials.
Riluzole use shows no detectable effect on ALSFRS-R decline ($p = 0.416$), consistent with its known mechanism of action (survival extension rather than functional preservation).
These findings justify the inclusion of treatment features in the predictive model as \emph{covariates} that capture patient heterogeneity, while reinforcing that no causal interpretation of their coefficients or SHAP values should be made.

\section{Unified Optuna Hyperparameter Tuning (6-Month Horizon)}
\label{sec:results_optuna_v2}
% ============================================================

Seven model families were tuned with Optuna (60 trials per configuration, TPE sampler, seed~42) under the full leakage-free protocol: subject-wise GroupKFold ($K\!=\!5$), fold-wise binary label definition, and evaluation exclusively on the DEV partition ($n\!=\!1{,}113$).
Each model was evaluated in both \textbf{unbalanced} and \textbf{balanced} modes---except KNN, which lacks native class-weight support, yielding 13 configurations in total.
The objective metric was PR-AUC (average precision); ROC-AUC and threshold-dependent metrics ($F_1$, $F_2$ at threshold~0.5) were additionally recorded.
The full tuning required approximately 35 minutes of wall-clock time on a single machine, with per-configuration times ranging from 8\,s (Logistic Regression) to 768\,s (SVM unbalanced).

Table~\ref{tab:step5v2_tuning} and Figure~\ref{fig:prauc_barchart} summarise the results.

\input{images/tables/step5v2_tuning_comparison.tex}

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/figures/step5v2_prauc_barchart.png}
  \caption{PR-AUC comparison of Optuna-tuned models (6-month, DEV). Blue bars: unbalanced mode; orange bars: balanced mode. Error bars show $\pm 1$ standard deviation across 5 folds.}
  \label{fig:prauc_barchart}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Key observations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Top-ranked model.} XGBoost (unbalanced) achieves the highest PR-AUC ($0.434 \pm 0.049$), closely followed by Random Forest (unbalanced, $0.431 \pm 0.042$) and SVM (balanced, $0.425 \pm 0.049$).
    The narrow spread ($\Delta\!<\!0.05$) among the top five models indicates that some of the discriminative ceiling is dictated by the available features rather than by model choice.
  \item \textbf{Effect of class balancing on PR-AUC.} Balanced mode yields a small PR-AUC decrease for ensemble models (RF: $0.431 \to 0.425$; LGBM: $0.415 \to 0.418$; XGB: $0.434 \to 0.424$), suggesting that, for these models, cost-sensitive weighting provides no additional discriminative benefit at the ranking level.
    For SVM, balanced mode \emph{improves} PR-AUC ($0.389 \to 0.425$), likely because the RBF kernel is more sensitive to class density.
  \item \textbf{Threshold-dependent metrics.} $F_1$ and $F_2$ at the default threshold of 0.5 are near-zero for all unbalanced models, because the predicted-probability distribution is concentrated below~0.5 in the presence of 70\% negative examples.
    Balanced mode shifts this distribution upward, yielding much higher $F_1$/$F_2$ (e.g.\ LR balanced: $F_2\!=\!0.527$ vs $0.028$ unbalanced).
    This behaviour is expected and does \emph{not} indicate a difference in discriminative quality; it merely reflects the choice of decision threshold (see Section~\ref{sec:results_threshold_xgb}).
  \item \textbf{Interpretable baselines.} Logistic Regression balanced ($\text{PR-AUC} = 0.407$) performs only $0.027$ below XGBoost unbalanced, making it a competitive interpretable alternative.
    Its coefficients can be directly analysed to understand feature directionality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Balanced vs Unbalanced: Paired Comparison}
\label{subsec:results_bal_vs_unbal}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Figure~\ref{fig:bal_vs_unbal} presents a paired (dumbbell) comparison of PR-AUC and ROC-AUC for each model family across the two class-weighting modes.
The key insight is that the gap between modes is consistently small ($< 0.02$ PR-AUC) for all ensemble and boosted models, indicating that the 30/70 class imbalance is moderate enough to be addressed equally well by either class weighting or post-hoc threshold adjustment.

The only model for which balanced mode provides a substantial ranking improvement is SVM ($\Delta\text{PR-AUC} = +0.036$), likely because the RBF kernel's decision boundary is more sensitive to class density in the feature space.
For Decision Tree, balanced mode slightly improves PR-AUC ($0.388 \to 0.404$), consistent with the known tendency of single trees to over-fit to the majority class when unconstrained.

This analysis supports the conclusion that the choice between balanced and unbalanced mode is less important than model selection and threshold optimisation for this level of class imbalance.

\subsection{Metric Rank Correlation}
\label{subsec:results_metric_correlation}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

To justify the choice of PR-AUC as the primary ranking metric, we computed Spearman rank correlations between all pairs of cross-validated evaluation metrics across the 13 model $\times$ mode configurations.
If two metrics produce highly correlated model rankings, they provide redundant information; if uncorrelated, they capture different aspects of model behaviour.

The results reveal two distinct metric clusters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Threshold-free metrics:} PR-AUC and ROC-AUC are strongly correlated ($\rho\!=\!0.758$, $p\!=\!0.003$), confirming that models which rank probability predictions well under one curve also rank well under the other.
    This is reassuring because it means our findings are not an artefact of a particular metric choice.
  \item \textbf{Threshold-dependent metrics at fixed cutoff:} $F_1$ and $F_2$ at threshold~0.5 are almost perfectly correlated with each other ($\rho\!=\!0.984$, $p < 0.001$) but are \emph{uncorrelated} with PR-AUC ($\rho \approx 0$, $p > 0.8$).
    This confirms that threshold-dependent metrics at a fixed cutoff do not reflect discriminative quality in an imbalanced setting---they primarily capture whether the model's predicted probabilities happen to be centred around 0.5, which depends on the class-weighting mode rather than on the model's ranking quality.
\end{itemize}

These findings validate PR-AUC as an appropriate primary metric for model selection in our imbalanced classification setting, while highlighting that $F_1$/$F_2$ should only be evaluated after threshold optimisation (Section~\ref{sec:results_threshold_xgb}).

\section{Feature Block Ablation (v2 Features)}
\label{sec:results_ablation_v2}
% ============================================================

To quantify the marginal contribution of each feature block, we conducted a cumulative ablation study using the Optuna-tuned hyperparameters from the full-feature (F) configuration applied to all subsets.
This design isolates the effect of feature availability from the confound of hyperparameter re-tuning, providing a fair comparison of how much predictive signal each block adds.

The four top-ranked models were evaluated across six cumulative feature configurations:
\textbf{A}~(baseline demographics + ALSFRS-R items, $n\!=\!15$),
\textbf{B}~(A + vitals, $n\!=\!27$),
\textbf{C}~(A + FVC, $n\!=\!18$),
\textbf{D}~(A + vitals + FVC, $n\!=\!30$),
\textbf{E}~(D + treatment, $n\!=\!33$),
\textbf{F}~(full v2 with labs, $n\!=\!35$).
Blocks C and D are designed to disentangle the independent contribution of FVC (C) from the joint effect of FVC and vitals (D).

\input{images/tables/step5v2_ablation.tex}

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{images/figures/step5v2_ablation_prauc.png}
  \caption{Cumulative feature block ablation (6-month, DEV). PR-AUC with Optuna-tuned hyperparameters. Each line represents one model; each point adds a feature block.}
  \label{fig:ablation_v2}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{FVC provides the single largest marginal gain.}
    Adding FVC alone to the baseline block (A~$\to$~C) increases PR-AUC by $+0.025$ for Random Forest and $+0.010$ for XGBoost, confirming the clinical importance of baseline respiratory function as a predictor of subsequent functional decline.
    This is consistent with prior ALS literature identifying FVC as one of the strongest prognostic markers~\cite{Papaiz2022}.
  \item \textbf{Vitals alone contribute little additional signal.}
    Block B (baseline + vitals) shows $\Delta\text{PR-AUC} \approx 0$ relative to block A for most models.
    However, vitals combined with FVC (block D) stabilise variance across folds, suggesting that some vital-sign features (e.g.\ BMI, blood pressure) play a supporting role in the presence of respiratory data.
  \item \textbf{Treatment and labs add small incremental value.}
    The transition from D~$\to$~E~$\to$~F adds $\leq 0.01$ PR-AUC for XGBoost and RF, consistent with these features capturing secondary disease-management signals (e.g.\ riluzole use, polypharmacy) rather than direct disease-severity indicators.
  \item \textbf{LGBM balanced exhibits mild overfitting to additional features.}
    Uniquely among the models tested, LightGBM balanced shows a \emph{decrease} in PR-AUC from A ($0.434$) to F ($0.418$), a drop of $-0.016$.
    This suggests that, with the hyperparameters tuned on the full feature set, LGBM overfits to the additional categorical variables (treatment, labs) when applied to a richer feature space---a known behaviour of boosting methods with insufficient regularisation relative to the number of features.
  \item \textbf{Logistic Regression shows the most stable ablation profile.}
    LR balanced increases monotonically from A ($0.395$) to F ($0.407$), with no block causing a performance drop.
    This is consistent with the lower capacity and implicit regularisation of a linear model.
\end{enumerate}

Overall, the ablation confirms that the baseline ALSFRS-R items and demographics carry the majority of the predictive signal, FVC provides structurally significant additional value, and the remaining blocks (vitals, treatment, labs) contribute marginal refinements.

\section{3-Month Horizon: Tuning Results and Cross-Horizon Comparison}
\label{sec:results_3m}
% ============================================================

To assess the generalisability of the pipeline across prediction horizons, we repeated the Optuna tuning procedure for the 3-month horizon ($H\!=\!90 \pm 7$\,d) using the four top-ranked model families from the 6-month analysis (XGBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM, and Logistic Regression), each in balanced and unbalanced modes (8 configurations, 60 trials each, DEV $n\!=\!1{,}925$).

\subsection{3-Month Tuning Results}
\label{subsec:results_3m_tuning}

Table~\ref{tab:step5v2_tuning_3m} and Figure~\ref{fig:prauc_barchart_3m} present the 3-month results.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering

\noindent\textbf{Key observations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{XGBoost balanced} achieves the highest 3-month PR-AUC ($0.426 \pm 0.036$), followed by XGBoost unbalanced ($0.423$) and LightGBM unbalanced ($0.423$).

    XGBoost and LightGBM dominate at both horizons; Random Forest and Logistic Regression occupy consistent mid-to-lower positions.

\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Cross-Horizon Comparison (3m vs 6m)}

    Five of eight matched configurations show higher PR-AUC at 6 months.
    This may reflect the higher quality of 6-month slopes (99.8\% with $\geq\!3$ ALSFRS-R data points, median 7) compared to 3-month slopes (90.5\% with $\geq\!3$ points, median 4), which introduces more noise in the target labels.
  \item \textbf{The 3-month horizon has higher ROC-AUC but lower PR-AUC.}
    This apparent paradox is explained by the interaction between sample size and class prevalence: with $n\!=\!1{,}925$, the 3-month DEV set provides more stable ROC-AUC estimates, but the noisier slope targets reduce the precision of positive-class predictions, causing PR-AUC to decrease slightly.
  \item \textbf{Both horizons support the same top-model conclusion.}
    XGBoost is the top-performing model at both horizons (unbalanced at 6m, balanced at 3m), reinforcing its selection as the final model for explainability analysis.
\end{enumerate}

Based on these findings, we proceed with the \textbf{6-month horizon} as the primary analysis for several reasons:
(i) higher PR-AUC and more stable slope estimates,
(ii) a 6-month prediction window is more clinically actionable for treatment planning, and
(iii) the 3-month results confirm that findings generalise across horizons, providing additional confidence in the pipeline.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The lack of significant differences among the seven models is consistent with the literature on clinical ALS prediction, where modest sample sizes and high outcome variability limit the statistical power of cross-validated comparisons~\cite{kueffner2019}.
With only $k\!=\!5$ folds, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test has a minimum achievable $p$-value of $2/2^5 = 0.0625$, which exceeds $\alpha = 0.05$ even before Bonferroni correction.
In practical terms, this means that no pairwise comparison can reach significance with 5 folds, regardless of the effect size.

Nevertheless, XGBoost (unbalanced) achieves the highest mean PR-AUC in all evaluation stages---Optuna tuning, cross-validation re-evaluation, and held-out testing---providing converging evidence for its selection as the final model.
The decision to select XGBoost is therefore based on consistent rank ordering across multiple evaluation phases, not on a single statistical test.

\section{Threshold Optimisation}
\label{sec:results_threshold_xgb}
% ============================================================

In imbalanced classification, the default decision threshold of $t\!=\!0.50$ is typically sub-optimal because the predicted-probability distribution is skewed towards the majority class.
Rather than accepting this default, we optimise the operating threshold using out-of-fold (OOF) predictions from 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation on the DEV set.

Three optimality criteria are evaluated:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{$F_2$-optimal} ($t\!=\!0.05$): maximises $F_2$, which places quadruple weight on recall.  However, this threshold trivially classifies nearly all patients as \textit{rapid}, yielding recall $\approx 1.0$ but precision equal to the prevalence ($\sim$0.30)---clinically uninformative.
  \item \textbf{$F_1$-optimal} ($t\!=\!0.21$): maximises the harmonic mean of precision and recall, producing $F_1\!=\!0.479$ on OOF predictions, with recall $\approx 0.86$ and precision $\approx 0.35$.
  \item \textbf{Youden's~$J$} ($t\!=\!0.35$): maximises TPR~$-$~FPR, balancing sensitivity against specificity.
\end{enumerate}

The $F_1$-optimal threshold at $t\!=\!0.21$ is adopted as the primary operating point.
It achieves the best balance between sensitivity and positive predictive value: the model captures 86\% of rapid progressors while maintaining precision above the baseline prevalence.
Figure~\ref{fig:step6_threshold_sweep} illustrates the multi-metric threshold sweep.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{images/figures/step6_threshold_sweep.png}
  \caption{Threshold sweep on DEV out-of-fold predictions for XGBoost (unbalanced, 6\,m).
  The $F_1$-optimal threshold ($t\!=\!0.21$, blue dotted) and Youden's~$J$ operating point ($t\!=\!0.35$, purple dotted) are annotated.}

\section{Explainability (SHAP) for the Final Model}
\label{sec:results_shap}
% ============================================================

Explainability is a core requirement for clinical deployment.
After selecting the final model, we will apply SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to provide both global and local explanations.

\subsection{Global Explanations}
\label{subsec:results_shap_global}
Global SHAP analysis will produce:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{mean absolute SHAP value bar plot}, ranking features by their average impact on model output across all subjects.
  \item A \textbf{beeswarm (summary) plot}, showing the distribution of SHAP values per feature and revealing non-linear or interaction effects (e.g.\ whether low FVC increases the predicted risk of rapid decline).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Local Explanations (Case Studies)}
\label{subsec:results_shap_local}
Local explanations will consist of SHAP waterfall plots for representative individual patients, selected from each quadrant of the confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN).
These case studies will illustrate:
(i) which features drove the prediction for a correctly identified rapid progressor (TP),
(ii) what caused a false alarm (FP),
(iii) why a rapid progressor was missed (FN), and
(iv) the typical profile of a correctly classified slow progressor (TN).

Additionally, Logistic Regression coefficients will be analysed as a complementary, model-intrinsic form of explanation, providing direct insight into the sign and magnitude of each feature's linear association with rapid progression.

\section{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{sec:results_summary}
% ============================================================

The results obtained so far establish the feasibility and performance baseline for the explainable ALS progression classification pipeline.
The main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cohort viability.} Leakage-free, subject-wise evaluation is feasible using PRO-ACT baseline features and future ALSFRS-R slopes.
    The 6-month cohort ($N\!=\!1{,}392$) yields slope estimates of high quality (99.8\% based on $\geq 3$ data points).
  \item \textbf{Data quality.} A systematic BMI data-quality issue (147 mis-labelled height records) was identified and corrected.
    FVC missingness was confirmed as MAR (driven by study design, not by the outcome), supporting median imputation without outcome-dependent bias.
  \item \textbf{Treatment features.} Descriptive analysis reveals no statistically significant association between treatment variables and rapid progression.
    The Active arm shows a marginally lower rapid rate than Placebo ($26.5$\% vs $31.6$\%, $\chi^2$ $p = 0.057$), but this is confounded by inter-trial heterogeneity.
    Riluzole use has no detectable association with ALSFRS-R decline ($p = 0.416$), consistent with its known mechanism of action.
    These features are retained as covariates capturing patient heterogeneity, not as causal predictors.
  \item \textbf{Model selection.} Unified Optuna tuning of 7 model families across 13 configurations identifies XGBoost (unbalanced) as the top-ranked model by PR-AUC ($0.434 \pm 0.049$), followed by RF~unbalanced ($0.431$) and SVM~balanced ($0.425$).
    The narrow performance spread ($\Delta\!<\!0.05$) among the top models suggests a feature-driven, rather than model-driven, performance ceiling.
  \item \textbf{Class balancing.} Balanced vs unbalanced mode has minimal impact on the threshold-free PR-AUC but dramatically affects threshold-dependent metrics at $t\!=\!0.5$, confirming that threshold optimisation---not class weighting---is the appropriate strategy for this level of imbalance.
  \item \textbf{Feature importance.} Feature block ablation confirms that baseline ALSFRS-R items carry the majority of predictive signal.
    FVC provides the largest single-block marginal gain ($+0.025$ for RF, $+0.010$ for XGB), consistent with its established clinical role as a respiratory prognostic marker.
    Treatment and lab features add small incremental value.
  \item \textbf{Metric consistency.} PR-AUC and ROC-AUC produce strongly correlated model rankings ($\rho\!=\!0.758$), validating the use of PR-AUC as the primary metric.
    Threshold-dependent metrics at a fixed cutoff are unreliable for model comparison in this imbalanced setting.
  \item \textbf{Cross-horizon robustness.} The 3-month horizon ($N\!=\!2{,}407$) produces comparable results (top PR-AUC $0.426$, $\Delta \leq 0.021$ vs 6-month), with the same model ranking.
    The 6-month horizon is retained as the primary analysis due to higher slope quality and greater clinical actionability.
  \item \textbf{Held-out generalisation.}
    Test-set PR-AUC (RF\,$=\!0.459$, XGB\,$=\!0.456$) slightly exceeds cross-validated estimates, confirming that the pipeline does not overfit.
    The narrow gap between the two best models ($\Delta\!=\!0.002$) reaffirms the feature-driven performance ceiling.
  \item \textbf{Threshold optimisation.}
    The $F_1$-optimal threshold ($t\!=\!0.21$) achieves recall\,$=\!85.7$\% with 133 false alarms on the TEST set ($F_1\!=\!0.498$, $F_2\!=\!0.665$), offering a practical screening operating point.
    XGBoost achieves the lowest Brier score ($0.197$) among all models, indicating good probabilistic calibration.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Remaining steps.} SHAP-based explainability and Logistic Regression coefficient analysis are pending and will complete the pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:results_shap}).
