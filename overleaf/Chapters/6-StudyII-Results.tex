\chapter{Survival Prognosis Replication: Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:study2_results}

This chapter presents and discusses the results of Study~II. It begins with the final cohort and model-selection results, followed by held-out test performance and threshold analysis. The later sections examine the BalancedBagging comparison, interpret the selected models using SHAP, and discuss the main limitations and clinical implications.

% ============================================================
\section{Cohort and Class Distribution}
\label{sec:s2r_cohort}
% ============================================================

The cohort derivation described in Section~\ref{subsec:s2_cohort} yielded 1,502 patients with complete data across all 23 predictors: 174 Short Survivors (11.6\%) and 1,328 Standard Survival patients (88.4\%).
The train/test partition yields 1,201 training patients (139 Short Survivors, 1,062 Standard Survival) and 301 test patients (35 Short Survivors, 266 Standard Survival).

This imbalance ratio of 7.6:1 is substantially more severe than the 2.3:1 ratio of Study~I.
A classifier that labels all patients as Standard Survival achieves 88.4\% accuracy while detecting zero Short Survivors.
Accuracy is therefore uninformative in isolation. ROC-AUC avoids dependence on a single decision threshold by evaluating ranking performance, but it must still be considered alongside sensitivity, specificity, and precision--recall measures when selecting an operating point.

MLP produced the highest mean ROC-AUC (0.915), followed by LightGBM+ROS (0.914) and SVM+SMOTEENN (0.911). These differences are small relative to the variation across folds, so the ordering should not be interpreted as evidence that MLP was consistently better than the other two models. SVM consequently achieved the highest G-Mean in the table (0.842), despite ranking third by ROC-AUC.

The selection rule had a similar effect within individual classifier families. For MLP, SMOTE obtained a mean ROC-AUC of 0.914, less than 0.002 below the selected unbalanced configuration, while increasing sensitivity from 0.427 to 0.835 and G-Mean from 0.641 to 0.837. This distinction is central to the interpretation of Study~II. The selected configurations are those with the strongest average ranking performance, not necessarily those with the most favourable sensitivity--specificity balance at a threshold of 0.5. The held-out evaluation therefore retains both threshold-free and threshold-dependent metrics rather than treating the cross-validation ranking as a final model comparison.

\section{AUC--Sensitivity Decoupling}
\label{sec:s2r_decoupling}
% ============================================================

The MLP detected only one of the 35 Short Survivors at the default threshold, giving a sensitivity of 0.029 and a balanced accuracy of 0.514. Its perfect specificity reflects an almost entirely negative classification rule rather than balanced performance: 34 Short Survivors were assigned to the Standard Survival class, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:s2_cm}. At the same time, its test ROC-AUC was 0.918, the third highest among the seven classifiers.

This is an example of ROC-AUC and operating-point performance becoming decoupled. ROC-AUC measures how well the scores rank Short Survivors above Standard Survival patients across all possible thresholds. It does not require the minority-class scores to exceed 0.50. The MLP retained strong ranking ability, but its probability scale placed almost every test patient below the default boundary. The cross-validation results already indicated this mismatch through the combination of ROC-AUC 0.915 and sensitivity 0.427; the test set showed a more extreme version of the same behaviour.

The precision--recall curves in Figure~\ref{fig:s2_pr} provide a second threshold-free view. MLP reached an average precision of 0.669, compared with 0.704 for LightGBM+ROS and 0.595 for SVM+SMOTEENN. Its ranking signal was therefore not absent. The confusion matrix, rather than the precision--recall curve, shows the consequence of applying the 0.50 threshold.

As in Study~I (Section~\ref{sec:results_threshold_xgb}), ranking performance and the final classification rule answer different questions. In the intended prognostic setting, a false negative could delay recognition of a patient at risk of short survival. A high ROC-AUC is therefore insufficient unless the operating threshold produces an acceptable balance between missed Short Survivors and false alerts.

\section{Decision Threshold Analysis}
\label{sec:s2r_threshold}
% ============================================================

The AUC--sensitivity gap motivates a systematic investigation of alternative decision thresholds.
For each classifier, three thresholds were derived from out-of-fold predictions generated during repeated cross-validation. This kept the held-out test set outside threshold selection. The thresholds were then applied to the test set:
(i)~Youden's J~\cite{youden1950} (maximises sensitivity $+$ specificity $- 1$);
(ii)~sensitivity-constrained (highest threshold achieving $\geq 0.90$ sensitivity on OOF predictions);
and (iii)~$F_1$-optimal (maximises minority-class $F_1$ on OOF predictions).
Table~\ref{tab:s2_threshold} reports results for the two most instructive classifiers.

TP/FN counts refer to the 35 Short Survivors.}

Threshold selection substantially changed the interpretation of the MLP. Youden's threshold increased sensitivity from 0.029 to 0.486 while retaining specificity of 0.966. The sensitivity-constrained threshold produced the largest recovery, identifying 27 of the 35 Short Survivors with specificity of 0.883. Its test sensitivity was 0.771 rather than the 0.90 targeted in the OOF data, showing that an operating constraint estimated during development does not necessarily transfer exactly to unseen patients. The $F_1$-optimal threshold was more conservative and identified 13 Short Survivors.

LightGBM+ROS was less sensitive to threshold adjustment. Youden's threshold identified the same 30 Short Survivors as the default while adding 14 false positives. The sensitivity-constrained threshold recovered one additional Short Survivor but reduced specificity from 0.831 to 0.759. In contrast, the $F_1$-optimal threshold favoured specificity, identifying 28 Short Survivors with specificity of 0.925 and the highest G-Mean in the table (0.860).

The MLP result shows that its failure at 0.50 was largely an operating-point problem rather than a complete failure to rank the minority class. LightGBM+ROS retained the stronger sensitivity--specificity balance at the default threshold, but threshold selection narrowed the practical difference between the models. Choosing among these operating points would require an explicit clinical cost for false negatives and false positives; the present analysis describes the trade-offs but does not define that cost.

BalancedBagging increased balanced accuracy for six of the seven classifiers. The largest gain was observed for SVM, from 0.500 to 0.793, followed by KNN and Random Forest. These tests describe the consistency of the paired differences within the repeated cross-validation procedure. Since training sets overlap across folds and repeats, the 15 observations are not fully independent. The adjusted p-values should therefore be interpreted as supporting evidence within this resampling protocol rather than as external validation.

\section{SHAP-Based Interpretation}
\label{sec:s2r_shap}
% ============================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:s2_shap} presents the TreeSHAP analysis of LightGBM+ROS on the held-out test set. Mean absolute SHAP values describe the average magnitude of each feature's contribution, while the beeswarm plot shows whether individual feature values moved predictions towards Short or Standard Survival.
\clearpage
\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/figures/step7_lightgbm_fig3_combined.png}
  \caption[SHAP feature importance: LightGBM+ROS (Study II)]{TreeSHAP analysis of LightGBM+ROS on the 301 held-out test patients. \textit{Left:} features ranked by mean absolute SHAP value. \textit{Right:} patient-level contributions, where positive values move the model output towards Short Survival and negative values towards Standard Survival. Colour represents the encoded feature value from low (blue) to high (red).}
  \label{fig:s2_shap}
\end{figure}

\texttt{Diagnosis\_Delay} had the largest mean absolute contribution (0.986), approximately twice that of Q8 walking slope (0.495). Short diagnostic delays moved predictions towards Short Survival, whereas long delays moved them strongly towards Standard Survival. This pattern is compatible with diagnostic delay acting as a proxy for early disease tempo~\cite{Feldman2022}, but it should not be interpreted as a causal effect. Both diagnostic delay and the survival outcome are measured from symptom onset, so part of this association also reflects their shared temporal anchor and the fixed 24-month target definition.

Four ALSFRS-R slopes appeared among the six highest-ranked features: Q8 walking, Q4 handwriting, Q1 speech, and Q10 respiratory function. Rapid decline categories generally increased the model output for Short Survival. Higher age-at-onset categories showed the same direction and ranked fifth (mean $|\text{SHAP}|=0.364$). Abnormal FVC at diagnosis also moved predictions towards Short Survival, although it ranked ninth (0.260). These are associations learned by the fitted LightGBM model from discretised predictors; they do not establish that changing any one feature would alter survival.

The overlap with Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024} is strongest at the level of the broader prognostic pattern. Both analyses emphasised short diagnostic delay, functional decline in walking, climbing stairs, and speech, older age, sex, and abnormal FVC. The detailed rankings were not identical. Q4 handwriting and Q10 respiratory slope were more prominent here, while Q6 dressing and hygiene was among the leading features in the reference study but ranked lower in the present LightGBM analysis.

KernelSHAP for the MLP also ranked \texttt{Diagnosis\_Delay} first, followed by Q4 handwriting, Q9 climbing stairs, Q8 walking, and Q2 salivation slopes. Agreement between the two fitted models was therefore partial rather than complete: for example, age at onset ranked fifth for LightGBM but fourteenth for MLP, while site of onset ranked sixth for MLP and twenty-first for LightGBM. The absolute SHAP magnitudes should not be compared across the two models. TreeSHAP explains the LightGBM output on its internal scale, whereas KernelSHAP approximates MLP probability contributions using 100 background centroids and 200 samples per patient.

Across all 23 features, the rank correlation between the two importance lists was moderate but did not reach the conventional 0.05 significance level (Spearman's $\rho=0.389$, $p=0.066$). This is consistent with a shared emphasis on a small group of predictors rather than agreement across the full ranking.

The distributions of the two leading LightGBM features are consistent with these attributions. Among Short Survivors, 66.7\% had a diagnostic delay of at most eight months, compared with 28.1\% of Standard Survival patients. Rapid Q8 walking decline occurred in 47.7\% and 5.0\% of the two groups, respectively. These summaries use the same cohort as the modelling analysis and are descriptive rather than independent support for the SHAP findings; the complete figure is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:study2_feature_distributions}.

\section{Clinical Interpretation and Scope}
\label{sec:s2r_clinical}
% ============================================================

The models estimate the probability of death within 24 months of symptom onset using information available at or near diagnosis. This is not equivalent to predicting survival for 24 months after diagnosis. A patient diagnosed 18 months after onset has only six months remaining before the target boundary, whereas a patient diagnosed after six months has 18. This distinction helps explain the dominant role of diagnostic delay and limits how the output should be communicated: it is a fixed onset-based risk classification, not an individual estimate of remaining survival time.

Within that definition, LightGBM+ROS provided the most balanced default operating point, while SVM+SMOTEENN favoured sensitivity at the cost of more false positives. The threshold analysis showed that this trade-off can change without retraining the model. A clinical operating point would therefore have to be selected from an explicit use case, such as deciding whether a high-risk prediction triggers closer review, and would require costs to be assigned to missed Short Survivors and unnecessary alerts. The present study did not define those costs.

SHAP explanations can indicate which recorded variables drove a model output, but they do not turn the classifier into a clinical decision rule. In particular, diagnostic delay and ALSFRS-R slopes partly summarise disease tempo already observed by diagnosis. Their importance may make a prediction easier to inspect, but it does not show that acting on those variables would change survival. The appropriate interpretation is therefore methodological: the models identify risk patterns in this PRO-ACT cohort and expose the consequences of imbalance and threshold choice. They are not validated for treatment selection, prognostic counselling, or deployment as a clinical decision-support system.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:s2r_limitations}
% ============================================================

The largest data limitation is the complete-case requirement. Only 1,502 of the 5,802 patients remaining after outcome and site-of-onset filtering had all 23 predictors available. FVC was missing for 62.3\% of these records and BMI for 20.0\%, with additional losses across the remaining variables. The analysed cohort may therefore differ systematically from patients with less complete trial follow-up. This selection cannot be corrected by the subsequent train/test split and limits both comparison with Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024} and generalisation to the wider ALS population.

All patients came from the same PRO-ACT extract, which combines clinical-trial populations rather than a population-based registry. The held-out test set prevents direct reuse of training patients, but it does not test transport to another registry, region, recruitment setting, or period. Moreover, only 35 Short Survivors were available for final evaluation. One prediction changes sensitivity by 2.86 percentage points, and the bootstrap interval for LightGBM+ROS sensitivity spans 0.724--0.967. These bootstrap intervals are conditional on the fitted models and selected thresholds; they do not include uncertainty from repeating cohort construction, hyperparameter optimisation, or model fitting on a new sample.

The fixed 24-month binary target simplifies censoring. Patients alive with less than 24 months of observed follow-up were excluded because their class was unresolved, while patients known to survive beyond 24 months were assigned to Standard Survival regardless of what occurred later. This is consistent with the replication target, but it discards information about survival beyond the boundary and may select for patients with sufficiently long recorded follow-up. A time-to-event analysis could retain censored observations and address a different, more detailed prognostic question.

Temporal alignment is also imperfect. For FVC, BMI, and ALSFRS assessments, the closest available record was selected by absolute distance from diagnosis and could occur after diagnosis. The resulting predictors are therefore best described as measurements at or near diagnosis, not strictly baseline information known at the diagnosis date. This matters most if the model is interpreted as a tool intended for use at that exact clinical encounter.

The feature representation was inherited to preserve comparison with the reference study. Continuous measurements were reduced to broad ordinal or binary categories, which removes within-category variation and makes the chosen cut-offs part of the model definition. The same design also fitted the MinMax scaler on the complete cohort before the train/test split. This is a preprocessing leakage because test-set feature ranges were available during scaling. In this dataset, every predictor had the same minimum and maximum in the training partition as in the complete cohort, so refitting the scaler on training data would have produced the same transformed values. The ordering should nevertheless be corrected in any future implementation.

Finally, the statistical comparisons do not constitute independent confirmation. Repeated cross-validation folds overlap in their training observations, and the Wilcoxon tests treat the 15 paired fold results as the comparison units. The resulting adjusted $p$-values describe consistency within this resampling design. External validation, preferably with prospectively defined thresholds and a prespecified clinical objective, would be required before drawing conclusions about real-world performance.

% ============================================================
\section{Summary}
\label{sec:s2r_summary}
% ============================================================

Study~II reproduced the main qualitative BalancedBagging pattern reported by Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024}. Balanced accuracy increased for four of the five directly comparable classifier families, with Naïve Bayes again providing the exception. Random Forest and LightGBM extend rather than reproduce the original comparison, and the reported performance values are not directly interchangeable across the two studies.

Selection by mean ROC-AUC retained configurations with similar ranking performance but very different behaviour at the default threshold. On the held-out test set, LightGBM+ROS provided the strongest balance at 0.50, with ROC-AUC 0.923, sensitivity 0.857, and specificity 0.831. The MLP reached ROC-AUC 0.918 but identified only one of 35 Short Survivors. Applying the sensitivity-constrained threshold selected from OOF predictions raised its test sensitivity to 0.771 while retaining specificity of 0.883. The discrepancy was therefore largely tied to the operating point rather than to an absence of ranking signal.

Both SHAP analyses ranked diagnostic delay first and placed several ALSFRS-R slopes among the leading predictors. Agreement across the complete feature rankings was only moderate, and the explanations remain descriptions of fitted-model behaviour. Together with the complete-case cohort, small minority-class test sample, onset-based target, and absence of external validation, this limits any clinical interpretation.

The main contribution of Study~II is consequently methodological. It shows why a replication claim must distinguish common findings from protocol changes, and why imbalance handling, ranking metrics, threshold selection, uncertainty, and model explanations must be examined together before performance can be interpreted.
