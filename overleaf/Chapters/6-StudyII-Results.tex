\chapter{Survival Prognosis Replication: Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:study2_results}

This chapter presents and discusses the results of Study~II. It begins with the final cohort and model-selection results, followed by held-out test performance and threshold analysis. The later sections examine the BalancedBagging comparison, interpret the selected models using SHAP, and discuss the main limitations and clinical implications.

% ============================================================
\section{Cohort and Class Distribution}
\label{sec:s2r_cohort}
% ============================================================

The cohort derivation described in Section~\ref{subsec:s2_cohort} yielded 1,502 patients with complete data across all 23 predictors: 174 Short Survivors (11.6\%) and 1,328 Standard Survival patients (88.4\%).
The train/test partition yields 1,201 training patients (139 Short Survivors, 1,062 Standard Survival) and 301 test patients (35 Short Survivors, 266 Standard Survival).

This imbalance ratio of 7.6:1 is substantially more severe than the 2.3:1 ratio of Study~I.
A classifier that labels all patients as Standard Survival achieves 88.4\% accuracy while detecting zero Short Survivors.
Accuracy is therefore uninformative in isolation. ROC-AUC avoids dependence on a single decision threshold by evaluating ranking performance, but it must still be considered alongside sensitivity, specificity, and precision--recall measures when selecting a clinically meaningful operating point.

MLP produced the highest mean ROC-AUC (0.915), followed by LightGBM+ROS (0.914) and SVM+SMOTEENN (0.911). These differences are small relative to the variation across folds, so the ordering should not be interpreted as evidence that MLP was consistently better than the other two models. Their behaviour at the default threshold was also markedly different. MLP identified 42.7\% of Short Survivors, whereas LightGBM+ROS identified 78.0\% and SVM+SMOTEENN 90.4\%. SVM consequently achieved the highest G-Mean in the table (0.842), despite ranking third by ROC-AUC.

The selection rule had a similar effect within individual classifier families. For MLP, SMOTE obtained a mean ROC-AUC of 0.914, less than 0.002 below the selected unbalanced configuration, while increasing sensitivity from 0.427 to 0.835 and G-Mean from 0.641 to 0.837. Random Forest showed the same pattern: ROS reduced mean ROC-AUC from 0.906 to 0.905 but increased sensitivity from 0.276 to 0.736. Since ROC-AUC was the optimisation criterion, neither resampled alternative was selected.

This distinction is central to the interpretation of Study~II. The selected configurations are those with the strongest average ranking performance, not necessarily those with the most favourable sensitivity--specificity balance at a threshold of 0.5. The held-out evaluation therefore retains both threshold-free and threshold-dependent metrics rather than treating the cross-validation ranking as a final model comparison.

% ============================================================
\section{AUC--Sensitivity Decoupling}
\label{sec:s2r_decoupling}
% ============================================================

The MLP detected only one of the 35 Short Survivors at the default threshold, giving a sensitivity of 0.029 and a balanced accuracy of 0.514. Its perfect specificity reflects an almost entirely negative classification rule rather than balanced performance: 34 Short Survivors were assigned to the Standard Survival class, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:s2_cm}. At the same time, its test ROC-AUC was 0.918, the third highest among the seven classifiers.

This is an example of ROC-AUC and operating-point performance becoming decoupled. ROC-AUC measures how well the scores rank Short Survivors above Standard Survival patients across all possible thresholds. It does not require the minority-class scores to exceed 0.50. The MLP retained strong ranking ability, but its probability scale placed almost every test patient below the default boundary. The cross-validation results already indicated this mismatch through the combination of ROC-AUC 0.915 and sensitivity 0.427; the test set showed a more extreme version of the same behaviour.

The precision--recall curves in Figure~\ref{fig:s2_pr} provide a second threshold-free view. MLP reached an average precision of 0.669, compared with 0.704 for LightGBM+ROS and 0.595 for SVM+SMOTEENN. Its ranking signal was therefore not absent. The confusion matrix, rather than the precision--recall curve, shows the consequence of applying the 0.50 threshold.

LightGBM+ROS and SVM+SMOTEENN correctly identify the majority of Short Survivors (TP), whereas MLP+None effectively classifies all patients as Standard Survival (FN\,$= 34$).}

As in Study~I (Section~\ref{sec:results_threshold_xgb}), ranking performance and the final classification rule answer different questions. In the intended prognostic setting, a false negative could delay recognition of a patient at risk of short survival. A high ROC-AUC is therefore insufficient unless the operating threshold produces an acceptable balance between missed Short Survivors and false alerts.

\section{Decision Threshold Analysis}
\label{sec:s2r_threshold}
% ============================================================

The AUC--sensitivity gap motivates a systematic investigation of alternative decision thresholds.
Three thresholds were derived from out-of-fold (OOF) CV predictions for each classifier---ensuring no information from the held-out test set was used in their selection---and subsequently applied to the test set:
(i)~Youden's J~\cite{youden1950} (maximises sensitivity $+$ specificity $- 1$);
(ii)~sensitivity-constrained (highest threshold achieving $\geq 0.90$ sensitivity on OOF predictions);
and (iii)~F1-optimal (maximises minority-class F1 on OOF predictions).
Table~\ref{tab:s2_threshold} reports results for the two most instructive classifiers.

The sensitivity-constrained threshold was the highest OOF value achieving sensitivity $\geq 0.90$; this constraint was not guaranteed to transfer unchanged to the held-out test set. TP/FN counts refer to the 35 Short Survivors.}
  \label{tab:s2_threshold}
  \resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}%

\end{table}

Threshold selection substantially changed the interpretation of the MLP. Youden's threshold increased sensitivity from 0.029 to 0.486 while retaining specificity of 0.966. The sensitivity-constrained threshold produced the largest recovery, identifying 27 of the 35 Short Survivors with specificity of 0.883. Its test sensitivity was 0.771 rather than the 0.90 targeted in the OOF data, showing that an operating constraint estimated during development does not necessarily transfer exactly to unseen patients. The $F_1$-optimal threshold was more conservative and identified 13 Short Survivors.

LightGBM+ROS was less sensitive to threshold adjustment. Youden's threshold identified the same 30 Short Survivors as the default while adding 14 false positives. The sensitivity-constrained threshold recovered one additional Short Survivor but reduced specificity from 0.831 to 0.759. In contrast, the $F_1$-optimal threshold favoured specificity, identifying 28 Short Survivors with specificity of 0.925 and the highest G-Mean in the table (0.860).

The MLP result shows that its failure at 0.50 was largely an operating-point problem rather than a complete failure to rank the minority class. LightGBM+ROS retained the stronger sensitivity--specificity balance at the default threshold, but threshold selection narrowed the practical difference between the models. Choosing among these operating points would require an explicit clinical cost for false negatives and false positives; the present analysis describes the trade-offs but does not define that cost.

BalancedBagging increased balanced accuracy for six of the seven classifiers. The largest gain was observed for SVM, from 0.500 to 0.793, followed by KNN and Random Forest. MLP achieved the highest mean balanced accuracy under BalancedBagging (0.832), with LightGBM (0.820) and Random Forest (0.814) close behind.

The effect was not a simultaneous improvement in both classes. Across all seven classifiers, BalancedBagging increased sensitivity and reduced specificity, shifting the predictions towards the minority class. For Naïve Bayes, these changes nearly cancelled each other: sensitivity increased, specificity decreased, and balanced accuracy rose by only 0.012. Its balanced-accuracy difference was therefore not significant after correction.

These tests describe the consistency of the paired differences within the repeated cross-validation procedure. Since training sets overlap across folds and repeats, the 15 observations are not fully independent. The adjusted p-values should therefore be interpreted as supporting evidence within this resampling protocol rather than as external validation.

The closest comparison concerns the five classifier families evaluated with BalancedBagging in both studies: KNN, Naïve Bayes, Decision Tree, SVM, and MLP. \section{SHAP-Based Interpretation}
\label{sec:s2r_shap}
% ============================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:s2_shap} presents the TreeSHAP analysis of LightGBM+ROS on the held-out test set. Mean absolute SHAP values describe the average magnitude of each feature's contribution, while the beeswarm plot shows whether individual feature values moved predictions towards Short or Standard Survival.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/figures/step7_lightgbm_fig3_combined.png}
  \caption[SHAP feature importance: LightGBM+ROS (Study II)]{TreeSHAP analysis of LightGBM+ROS on the 301 held-out test patients. \textit{Left:} features ranked by mean absolute SHAP value. \textit{Right:} patient-level contributions, where positive values move the model output towards Short Survival and negative values towards Standard Survival. Colour represents the encoded feature value from low (blue) to high (red).}
  \label{fig:s2_shap}
\end{figure}

\texttt{Diagnosis\_Delay} had the largest mean absolute contribution (0.986), approximately twice that of Q8 walking slope (0.495). Short diagnostic delays moved predictions towards Short Survival, whereas long delays moved them strongly towards Standard Survival. This pattern is compatible with diagnostic delay acting as a proxy for early disease tempo~\cite{Feldman2022}, but it should not be interpreted as a causal effect. Both diagnostic delay and the survival outcome are measured from symptom onset, so part of this association also reflects their shared temporal anchor and the fixed 24-month target definition.

Four ALSFRS-R slopes appeared among the six highest-ranked features: Q8 walking, Q4 handwriting, Q1 speech, and Q10 respiratory function. Rapid decline categories generally increased the model output for Short Survival. Higher age-at-onset categories showed the same direction and ranked fifth (mean $|\text{SHAP}|=0.364$). Abnormal FVC at diagnosis also moved predictions towards Short Survival, although it ranked ninth (0.260). These are associations learned by the fitted LightGBM model from discretised predictors; they do not establish that changing any one feature would alter survival.

The overlap with Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024} is strongest at the level of the broader prognostic pattern. Both analyses emphasised short diagnostic delay, functional decline in walking, climbing stairs, and speech, older age, sex, and abnormal FVC. The detailed rankings were not identical. Q4 handwriting and Q10 respiratory slope were more prominent here, while Q6 dressing and hygiene was among the leading features in the reference study but ranked lower in the present LightGBM analysis.

KernelSHAP for the MLP also ranked \texttt{Diagnosis\_Delay} first, followed by Q4 handwriting, Q9 climbing stairs, Q8 walking, and Q2 salivation slopes. Agreement between the two fitted models was therefore partial rather than complete: for example, age at onset ranked fifth for LightGBM but fourteenth for MLP, while site of onset ranked sixth for MLP and twenty-first for LightGBM. The absolute SHAP magnitudes should not be compared across the two models. TreeSHAP explains the LightGBM output on its internal scale, whereas KernelSHAP approximates MLP probability contributions using 100 background centroids and 200 samples per patient.

Figure~\ref{fig:s2_featbars} provides a descriptive view of the two most prominent LightGBM features. Among Short Survivors, 66.7\% had a diagnostic delay of at most eight months, compared with 28.1\% of Standard Survival patients. Rapid Q8 walking decline occurred in 47.7\% and 5.0\% of the two groups, respectively. These outcome-stratified distributions help explain why the fitted model used the features, but they are not an independent validation of the SHAP results.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:s2r_limitations}
% ============================================================

Several limitations apply to the results of Study~II.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Smaller cohort.} The 1,502-patient cohort is 23.6\% smaller than Papaiz et al.'s 1,967, primarily due to FVC missingness in the publicly available PRO-ACT extract (62.3\% missing post-filters). Patients with complete FVC records may differ systematically from those without, introducing potential selection bias.
  \item \textbf{Internal test set only.} Train and test sets are drawn from the same PRO-ACT extract. No external validation was performed. Results may not generalise to other clinical populations, different ALS registries, or healthcare systems with different referral patterns.
  \item \textbf{Small minority-class test set.} Only 35 Short Survivors appear in the test set. One patient equals 2.86 percentage points of sensitivity, making point estimates highly sensitive to individual predictions. Bootstrap confidence intervals confirm wide uncertainty (e.g., LightGBM+ROS recall CI: [0.724,\,0.967]).
  \item \textbf{Post-diagnosis ALSFRS-R slope risk.} Where no pre-diagnosis assessment was available, the nearest visit was used. For a subset of patients, this introduces limited post-diagnosis information into the predictor set, a constraint inherited from the reference study's design.
  \item \textbf{Feature set inherited from the reference study.} The 23 ordinal/binary features were chosen to match Papaiz et al.\ for direct comparison, not necessarily to maximise predictive performance. Alternative or richer feature representations may yield better discrimination.
  \item \textbf{Survival target depends on censoring assumptions.} Subjects alive at last observation with follow-up $> 24$ months are classified as Standard Survival, which may include patients who die shortly after. The 24-month threshold follows the reference study exactly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:s2r_summary}
% ============================================================

Study~II reproduced the qualitative BalancedBagging pattern reported by Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024}: balanced accuracy improved for most classifier families, while Naïve Bayes was the exception. The performance values are not directly comparable because the studies differ in evaluation stage and ensemble implementation.

Among the configurations evaluated in this study, LightGBM with Random Oversampling provided the strongest balance at the default threshold, with ROC-AUC 0.923, sensitivity 0.857, and specificity 0.831 on the held-out test set.

The most consequential finding is the AUC--sensitivity decoupling: the MLP achieved the third-highest test ROC-AUC (0.918) while detecting only 1 of 35 Short Survivors at the default threshold (sensitivity 0.029).
Threshold adjustment changed that interpretation. The sensitivity-constrained OOF threshold increased test sensitivity to 0.771 while retaining specificity of 0.883, showing that the default probability boundary was poorly aligned with the intended minority-class detection task.

Both SHAP analyses ranked \texttt{Diagnosis\_Delay} first and placed several ALSFRS-R slopes among the leading predictors, but the detailed rankings differed between LightGBM and MLP. The shared pattern also overlaps partly with the interpretation reported in the reference study.

Taken together, Study~II demonstrates that imbalance-aware learning can improve ALS survival prognosis, while also showing that high ROC-AUC can mask clinically unacceptable minority-class recall.
These findings reinforce the dissertation's broader argument: ALS prognostic modelling must be evaluated through threshold behaviour, minority-class sensitivity, and interpretability, not ranking metrics alone.
