﻿\chapter{Survival Prognosis Replication: Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:study2_results}

This chapter presents and discusses the results of Study~II. It begins with the final cohort and model-selection results, followed by held-out test performance and threshold analysis. The later sections examine the BalancedBagging comparison, interpret the selected models using SHAP, and discuss the main limitations and clinical implications.

% ============================================================
\section{Cohort and Class Distribution}
\label{sec:s2r_cohort}
% ============================================================

The cohort derivation described in Section~\ref{subsec:s2_cohort} yielded 1,502 patients with complete data across all 23 predictors: 174 Short Survivors (11.6\%) and 1,328 Standard Survival patients (88.4\%).
The train/test partition yields 1,201 training patients (139 Short Survivors, 1,062 Standard Survival) and 301 test patients (35 Short Survivors, 266 Standard Survival).

This imbalance ratio of 7.6:1 is substantially more severe than the 2.3:1 ratio of Study~I.
A classifier that labels all patients as Standard Survival achieves 88.4\% accuracy while detecting zero Short Survivors.
Accuracy is therefore uninformative in isolation. ROC-AUC avoids dependence on a single decision threshold by evaluating ranking performance, but it must still be considered alongside sensitivity, specificity, and precision--recall measures when selecting an operating point.

\section{Probability Assessment}
\label{sec:s2r_calibration}

The probability assessment changes the interpretation of the ranking results (Table~\ref{tab:review_s2_probability_metrics}). LightGBM+ROS has the highest average precision, 0.704, but its Brier score is 0.1034, slightly worse than 0.1028 for a constant equal to test prevalence. Its mean predicted probability is 0.260 against an observed Short Survivor proportion of 0.116. This overestimation is consistent with training on an oversampled 50:50 class mix; high ROC-AUC does not make those probabilities calibrated risks.

Random Forest has the lowest Brier score (0.065), although its default operating point misses most Short Survivors. Conversely, the MLP's mean probability is only 0.054 despite ROC-AUC 0.918. These models expose different problems: ranking, probabilistic accuracy, and classification at a chosen boundary need separate assessment. The supplementary reliability diagrams in Figure~\ref{fig:review_s2_calibration} retain the original probabilities; no post-hoc recalibration was applied to improve the held-out scores.

\section{AUC--Sensitivity Decoupling}
\label{sec:s2r_decoupling}
% ============================================================

The MLP detected only one of the 35 Short Survivors at the default threshold, giving a sensitivity of 0.029 and a balanced accuracy of 0.514. Its perfect specificity reflects an almost entirely negative classification rule rather than balanced performance: 34 Short Survivors were assigned to the Standard Survival class, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:s2_cm}. At the same time, its test ROC-AUC was 0.918, the third highest among the seven classifiers.

This is an example of ROC-AUC and operating-point performance becoming decoupled. ROC-AUC measures how well the scores rank Short Survivors above Standard Survival patients across all possible thresholds. It does not require the minority-class scores to exceed 0.50. The MLP retained strong ranking ability, but its probability scale placed almost every test patient below the default boundary. The cross-validation results already indicated this mismatch through the combination of ROC-AUC 0.915 and sensitivity 0.427; the test set showed a more extreme version of the same behaviour.

The precision--recall curves in Figure~\ref{fig:s2_pr} provide a second threshold-free view. MLP reached an average precision of 0.669, compared with 0.704 for LightGBM+ROS and 0.595 for SVM+SMOTEENN. Its ranking signal was therefore not absent. The confusion matrix, rather than the precision--recall curve, shows the consequence of applying the 0.50 threshold.

\section{Decision Threshold Analysis}
\label{sec:s2r_threshold}
% ============================================================

The AUC--sensitivity gap motivates a systematic investigation of alternative decision thresholds.
For each classifier, three thresholds were derived from out-of-fold predictions generated during repeated cross-validation. This kept the held-out test set outside threshold selection. The thresholds were then applied to the test set:
(i)~Youden's J~\cite{youden1950} (maximises sensitivity $+$ specificity $- 1$);
(ii)~sensitivity-constrained (highest threshold achieving $\geq 0.90$ sensitivity on OOF predictions);
and (iii)~$F_1$-optimal (maximises minority-class $F_1$ on OOF predictions).
Table~\ref{tab:s2_threshold} reports results for the two most instructive classifiers.

TP/FN counts refer to the 35 Short Survivors.}

Threshold selection substantially changed the interpretation of the MLP. Youden's threshold increased sensitivity from 0.029 to 0.486 while retaining specificity of 0.966. The sensitivity-constrained threshold produced the largest recovery, identifying 27 of the 35 Short Survivors with specificity of 0.883. Its test sensitivity was 0.771 rather than the 0.90 targeted in the OOF data. LightGBM+ROS also missed that target, reaching 0.886. An operating constraint estimated during development therefore did not transfer exactly to the test sample for either model. The $F_1$-optimal threshold was more conservative and identified 13 Short Survivors.

LightGBM+ROS was less sensitive to threshold adjustment. Youden's threshold identified the same 30 Short Survivors as the default while adding 14 false positives. The sensitivity-constrained threshold recovered one additional Short Survivor but reduced specificity from 0.831 to 0.759. In contrast, the $F_1$-optimal threshold favoured specificity, identifying 28 Short Survivors with specificity of 0.925 and the highest G-Mean in the table (0.860).

The MLP result shows that its failure at 0.50 was largely an operating-point problem rather than a complete failure to rank the minority class. LightGBM+ROS retained the stronger sensitivity--specificity balance at the default threshold, but threshold selection narrowed the practical difference between the models. Choosing among these operating points would require an explicit clinical cost for false negatives and false positives; the present analysis describes the trade-offs but does not define that cost.

BalancedBagging increased mean balanced accuracy for all seven classifiers, with statistically significant differences for six after Bonferroni correction. The largest gain was observed for SVM, from 0.500 to 0.793, followed by KNN and Random Forest. MLP achieved the highest mean balanced accuracy under BalancedBagging (0.832), with LightGBM (0.820) and Random Forest (0.814) close behind.

The effect was not a simultaneous improvement in both classes. Across all seven classifiers, BalancedBagging increased sensitivity and reduced specificity, shifting the predictions towards the minority class. For Naïve Bayes, these changes nearly cancelled each other: sensitivity increased, specificity decreased, and balanced accuracy rose by only 0.012. Its balanced-accuracy difference was therefore not significant after correction.

These tests describe the consistency of the paired differences within the repeated cross-validation procedure. Since training sets overlap across folds and repeats, the 15 observations are not fully independent. The adjusted p-values should therefore be interpreted as supporting evidence within this resampling protocol rather than as external validation.

The closest comparison concerns the five classifier families evaluated with BalancedBagging in both studies: KNN, Naïve Bayes, Decision Tree, SVM, and MLP. In both analyses, the balanced ensemble significantly improved balanced accuracy for four of these five families; Naïve Bayes did not show a significant gain. This reproduces the qualitative pattern reported by Papaiz et al., although not the exact experimental protocol or performance values.

The supplementary ranking comparison narrows this claim. ROC-AUC increases for KNN (0.877 to 0.888) and Decision Tree (0.858 to 0.884), is almost unchanged for Naive Bayes, and decreases for the remaining four families. SVM is the clearest example: balanced accuracy rises from 0.500 to 0.793 while ROC-AUC falls from 0.910 to 0.880. BalancedBagging therefore improves default-operating-point classification in this experiment, but not consistently discrimination. These descriptive comparisons use the original configurations and folds (Table~\ref{tab:review_s2_bagging_auc}), whose overlapping training sets do not provide independent replications.

The reported neural-network balanced accuracy was 0.88 in the reference study, compared with a repeated cross-validation mean of 0.832 for MLP with BalancedBagging in the present analysis. These values come from different evaluation stages and should not be treated as a direct performance comparison. Differences in cohort composition, optimisation objective, hyperparameter search, and ensemble implementation may all contribute, and their individual effects cannot be separated here.

The Random Forest result is not an exact replication because Papaiz et al. used Balanced Random Forest, whereas the present study applied the same BalancedBagging wrapper used for the other classifiers. LightGBM was not included in the reference work and should instead be regarded as an extension. Overall, the results support the broader finding that balanced ensembles can recover minority-class sensitivity under severe imbalance, while also showing that the magnitude of the benefit depends on the classifier and evaluation protocol.

\section{SHAP-Based Interpretation}
\label{sec:s2r_shap}
% ============================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:s2_shap} presents the TreeSHAP analysis of LightGBM+ROS on the held-out test set. Mean absolute SHAP values describe the average magnitude of each feature's contribution, while the beeswarm plot shows whether individual feature values moved predictions towards Short or Standard Survival.
\clearpage
\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/figures/step7_lightgbm_fig3_combined.png}
  \caption[SHAP feature importance: LightGBM+ROS (Study II)]{TreeSHAP analysis of LightGBM+ROS on the 301 held-out test patients. \textit{Left:} features ranked by mean absolute SHAP value. \textit{Right:} patient-level contributions, where positive values move the model output towards Short Survival and negative values towards Standard Survival. Colour represents the encoded feature value from low (blue) to high (red).}
  \label{fig:s2_shap}
\end{figure}

\texttt{Diagnosis\_Delay} had the largest mean absolute contribution (0.986), approximately twice that of Q8 walking slope (0.495). Short diagnostic delays moved predictions towards Short Survival, whereas long delays moved them towards Standard Survival. Early disease tempo is one possible source of this association~\cite{Feldman2022}, but delayed trial entry is another: patients observed later after onset have less, or no, remaining time before the 24-month boundary. The audit in Table~\ref{tab:review_s2_delayed_entry} confirms a large difference in entry-time proxies between the classes. The contribution cannot be interpreted as a pure disease-tempo signal or a causal effect.

The LightGBM TreeSHAP scale is the raw model margin (log-odds). Random oversampling makes the training classes equally frequent, whereas Short Survivors account for 11.6\% of the observed cohort. The attributions therefore explain the oversampled model's score, not changes in calibrated clinical risk at the cohort prevalence.

Four ALSFRS-R slopes appeared among the six highest-ranked features: Q8 walking, Q4 handwriting, Q1 speech, and Q10 respiratory function. Rapid decline categories generally increased the model output for Short Survival. Higher age-at-onset categories showed the same direction and ranked fifth (mean $|\text{SHAP}|=0.364$). Abnormal FVC in the selected trial assessment also moved predictions towards Short Survival, although it ranked ninth (0.260). These are associations learned by the fitted LightGBM model from discretised predictors; they do not establish that changing any one feature would alter survival.

The overlap with Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024} is strongest at the level of the broader prognostic pattern. Both analyses emphasised short diagnostic delay, functional decline in walking, climbing stairs, and speech, older age, sex, and abnormal FVC. The detailed rankings were not identical. Q4 handwriting and Q10 respiratory slope were more prominent here, while Q6 dressing and hygiene was among the leading features in the reference study but ranked lower in the present LightGBM analysis.

KernelSHAP for the MLP also ranked \texttt{Diagnosis\_Delay} first, followed by Q4 handwriting, Q9 climbing stairs, Q8 walking, and Q2 salivation slopes. Agreement between the two fitted models was therefore partial rather than complete: for example, age at onset ranked fifth for LightGBM but fourteenth for MLP, while site of onset ranked sixth for MLP and twenty-first for LightGBM. The absolute SHAP magnitudes should not be compared across the two models. TreeSHAP explains the LightGBM output on its internal scale, whereas KernelSHAP approximates MLP probability contributions using 100 background centroids and 200 samples per patient.

Across all 23 features, the rank correlation between the two importance lists was moderate but did not reach the conventional 0.05 significance level (Spearman's $\rho=0.389$, $p=0.066$). This is consistent with a shared emphasis on a small group of predictors rather than agreement across the full ranking.

The distributions of the two leading LightGBM features are consistent with these attributions. Among Short Survivors, 66.7\% had a diagnostic delay of at most eight months, compared with 28.1\% of Standard Survival patients. Rapid Q8 walking decline occurred in 47.7\% and 5.0\% of the two groups, respectively. These summaries use the same cohort as the modelling analysis and are descriptive rather than independent support for the SHAP findings; the complete figure is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:study2_feature_distributions}.

\section{Temporal and Encoding Sensitivity}
\label{sec:s2r_temporal_sensitivity}

The supplementary analyses retain the preceding results as the replication reference rather than replacing them. Correcting BMI boundaries changes two coded values in the 1,502-patient cohort. LightGBM and MLP retain their reported ROC-AUC and native confusion matrices at the displayed precision, so this correction does not explain their original contrast.

Restricting the entry-time proxy to at most 18 months leaves 424 participants, including 165 Short Survivors. Under the original assignments, 91 remain in TEST, of whom 34 are Short Survivors. With the saved configurations refitted, LightGBM+ROS obtains ROC-AUC 0.828 (95\% CI: $[0.728,0.910]$), sensitivity 0.706 and specificity 0.789. MLP obtains ROC-AUC 0.823, sensitivity 0.735 and specificity 0.860. The original 0.923/0.918 ranking estimates therefore do not persist unchanged in the earlier-entry population. The smaller difference between the models' native sensitivities also shows that the original MLP operating-point failure is not a fixed property of that classifier family.

The time-bounded 18-month landmark asks a different question: death between months 18 and 24 among patients alive and observed at month 18. Of 344 initially eligible participants, only 161 retain complete predictors under the date restrictions. TEST contains 33 participants and five subsequent deaths. LightGBM+ROS reaches ROC-AUC 0.693 ($[0.507,0.863]$) and detects three deaths; MLP reaches 0.764 ($[0.600,0.910]$) and detects two. These are small, uncertain estimates, not a basis for selecting a new best model. Both models detect all five deaths at their development-derived sensitivity thresholds, but specificity falls to 0.321 for LightGBM and 0.107 for MLP.

The 12-month entry restriction is less informative than its average precision might suggest. Its test set contains 22 Short Survivors among only 28 patients, giving an AP reference prevalence of 0.786. LightGBM obtains ROC-AUC 0.576 despite AP 0.841; MLP obtains 0.765 and 0.941 but classifies every test patient as Short at its native rule. KNN+RUS is infeasible because one training fold has only 22 observations after undersampling for a fixed $k=31$. The dated 12-month landmark leaves 41 complete cases, only four without a subsequent death by month 24, and cannot support the planned five-fold comparison. Neither case was assigned a fallback score.

These contrasts show that the performance claim depends on when patients are observed and which measurements are permitted. They do not isolate a numerical amount of left-truncation bias: restriction changes the participants, class balance and fitted models, while landmarking also changes the prediction question. Tables~\ref{tab:review_s2_timed_cohorts}--\ref{tab:review_s2_landmark18} and Figure~\ref{fig:review_s2_timing_sensitivity} report the full comparisons.

\section{Clinical Interpretation and Scope}
\label{sec:s2r_clinical}
% ============================================================

The models classify death within 24 months of symptom onset using trial records that were generally collected after diagnosis. The temporal audit found median offsets of approximately ten months for the selected ALSFRS, FVC, and weight records, with more than 99\% of each source occurring after diagnosis. The task is therefore a retrospective fixed-horizon classification within the reconstructed PRO-ACT cohort, not a prediction made at diagnosis. It is also not equivalent to predicting survival for 24 months after diagnosis. A patient diagnosed 18 months after onset has only six months remaining before the target boundary, whereas a patient diagnosed after six months has 18.

Within that definition, LightGBM+ROS provided the most balanced default operating point, while SVM+SMOTEENN favoured sensitivity at the cost of more false positives. The threshold analysis showed that this trade-off can change without retraining the model. A clinical operating point would therefore have to be selected from an explicit use case, such as deciding whether a high-risk prediction triggers closer review, and would require costs to be assigned to missed Short Survivors and unnecessary alerts. The present study did not define those costs.

SHAP explanations can indicate which recorded variables drove a model output, but they do not turn the classifier into a clinical decision rule. In particular, diagnostic delay and ALSFRS-R slopes summarise disease tempo observed by the selected trial assessment. Their importance may make a prediction easier to inspect, but it does not show that acting on those variables would change survival. The appropriate interpretation is therefore methodological: the models identify risk patterns in this PRO-ACT cohort and expose the consequences of imbalance and threshold choice. They are not validated for treatment selection, prognostic counselling, or deployment as a clinical decision-support system.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:s2r_limitations}
% ============================================================

The largest data limitation is the complete-case requirement. Only 1,502 of the 5,802 patients remaining after outcome and site-of-onset filtering had all 23 predictors available. FVC was missing for 62.3\% of these records and BMI for 20.0\%, with additional losses across the remaining variables. The analysed cohort may therefore differ systematically from patients with less complete trial follow-up. This selection cannot be corrected by the subsequent train/test split and limits both comparison with Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024} and generalisation to the wider ALS population.

The observed comparison makes that selection more concrete (Table~\ref{tab:review_s2_selection}). Median age at onset is 54.0 years in complete cases and 55.5 in excluded participants. Among recorded FVC values, medians are 81.0\% and 89.4\%, but FVC is available for only 683 of the 4,300 excluded participants. Short Survivor proportions are 11.6\% and 10.2\%. These are selected observed-data summaries; the unmeasured FVC distribution in excluded patients remains unknown.

The expanded-cohort sensitivity analysis retained all 5,802 eligible patients, including 123 Short Survivors in its 1,161-patient test partition. With five iterative imputations, LightGBM+ROS obtained ROC-AUC 0.906, PR-AUC 0.523, sensitivity 0.797, and specificity 0.833. Median imputation on the same split gave ROC-AUC 0.903, PR-AUC 0.515, sensitivity 0.813, and specificity 0.828. Both estimates are lower than the complete-case values of 0.923 and 0.704 for ROC-AUC and PR-AUC, respectively. Predictive signal therefore remained in the expanded cohort, but the change in performance confirms that cohort selection matters. The analysis does not show which estimate would transfer better to an external population; Appendix~\ref{app:study2_missing_sensitivity} gives the full comparison.

All patients came from the same PRO-ACT extract, which combines clinical-trial populations rather than a population-based registry. The held-out test set prevents direct reuse of training patients, but it does not test transport to another registry, region, recruitment setting, or period. Moreover, only 35 Short Survivors were available for final evaluation. One prediction changes sensitivity by 2.86 percentage points, and the bootstrap interval for LightGBM+ROS sensitivity spans 0.724--0.967. These bootstrap intervals are conditional on the fitted models and selected thresholds; they do not include uncertainty from repeating cohort construction, hyperparameter optimisation, or model fitting on a new sample.

Training and test patients can also share a source trial. The split does not isolate trial-specific eligibility, treatment, measurement, or recording practices. No reliable trial-membership variable was established for a leave-trial-out analysis, and missingness patterns were not treated as verified trial identifiers. The internal holdout may therefore be optimistic for performance in a new trial.

The fixed 24-month binary target simplifies censoring. Patients alive with less than 24 months of observed follow-up were excluded because their class was unresolved, while patients known to survive beyond 24 months were assigned to Standard Survival regardless of what occurred later. This is consistent with the replication target, but it discards information about survival beyond the boundary and may select for patients with sufficiently long recorded follow-up. A time-to-event analysis could retain censored observations and address a different, more detailed prognostic question.

Left truncation is separate from censoring and from post-diagnosis predictor timing. The median onset-to-trial-reference interval is 11.2 months in Short Survivors and 26.1 months in Standard Survival patients; 756 of 1,328 Standard Survival patients (56.9\%) are already at or beyond 24 months at the reference day. Surviving to entry thus partly constrains the class before the model is fitted. The lower discrimination under entry restriction reinforces the limited scope of the original ROC-AUC. Landmarking makes predictor timing explicit and targets conditional future risk, but its complete-case selection and five test events do not establish unbiased prognosis in the wider ALS population. No delayed-entry survival model or trial-level validation was fitted.

Temporal alignment is a substantive limitation. The closest-record rule selected post-diagnosis measurements for 1,496 ALSFRS assessments, 1,492 FVC assessments, and 1,494 weight records in the 1,502-patient cohort. Median offsets were 314, 310.5, and 311 days. Only five patients had selected records at or before diagnosis for all three sources, so a strict pre-diagnosis analysis is not supported by this extract. The fitted models must not be interpreted as tools for use at the diagnosis encounter.

The feature representation was inherited to preserve comparison with the reference study. Continuous measurements were reduced to broad ordinal or binary categories, which removes within-category variation and makes the chosen cut-offs part of the model definition. In the final implementation, MinMax scaling is fitted after the train/test split and separately within each cross-validation fold. Every predictor had the same minimum and maximum in the complete cohort, training partition, and all 15 fold-training samples. The corrected train and test matrices were therefore identical to the previous matrices (maximum absolute difference 0), and the held-out metrics and confusion matrices did not change.

The historical search also has an audit limit: its aggregate exports cannot establish whether a failed fold ever received the original ROC-AUC fallback of 0.5. The revised code stops and records errors, but this does not retrospectively verify the selected configurations (Section~\ref{subsec:s2_optuna}). The new temporal fits have explicit failure and warning records; the primary searches were not repeated.

Finally, the statistical comparisons do not constitute independent confirmation. Repeated cross-validation folds overlap in their training observations, and the Wilcoxon tests treat the 15 paired fold results as the comparison units. The resulting adjusted $p$-values describe consistency within this resampling design. External validation, preferably with prospectively defined thresholds and a prespecified clinical objective, would be required before drawing conclusions about real-world performance.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:s2r_summary}
% ============================================================

Study~II reproduced the main qualitative BalancedBagging pattern reported by Papaiz et al.~\cite{Papaiz2024}. Balanced accuracy increased significantly for four of the five directly comparable classifier families, while the small increase for Naïve Bayes was not significant after correction. Random Forest and LightGBM extend rather than reproduce the original comparison, and the reported performance values are not directly interchangeable across the two studies.

Selection by mean ROC-AUC retained configurations with similar ranking performance but very different behaviour at the default threshold. On the held-out test set, LightGBM+ROS provided the strongest balance at 0.50, with ROC-AUC 0.923, sensitivity 0.857, and specificity 0.831. The MLP reached ROC-AUC 0.918 but identified only one of 35 Short Survivors. Applying the sensitivity-constrained threshold selected from OOF predictions raised its test sensitivity to 0.771 while retaining specificity of 0.883. The discrepancy was therefore largely tied to the operating point rather than to an absence of ranking signal.

Both SHAP analyses ranked diagnostic delay first and placed several ALSFRS-R slopes among the leading predictors. Agreement across the complete feature rankings was only moderate, and the explanations remain descriptions of fitted-model behaviour. The complete-case cohort, mostly post-diagnosis predictors, small minority-class test sample, onset-based target, and absence of external validation limit any clinical interpretation.

The temporal analyses narrow the performance claim further. Earlier-entry discrimination is lower, and enforcing a common prediction time leaves much smaller complete-case cohorts. The original results remain a retrospective replication reference; the revised tasks do not support diagnosis-time or clinical deployment claims.

The main contribution of Study~II is consequently methodological. It shows why a replication claim must distinguish common findings from protocol changes, and why imbalance handling, ranking metrics, threshold selection, uncertainty, and model explanations must be examined together before performance can be interpreted.
