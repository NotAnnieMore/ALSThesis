\chapter{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\section{Context}
\label{subsec:context}

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (\gls{ALS}) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterised by the progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons. Its clinical course includes muscle weakness, dysarthria, dysphagia, and, in later stages, respiratory failure \cite{Feldman2022}. Median survival is commonly estimated at two to four years from symptom onset, although individual trajectories differ considerably. Some patients deteriorate within one or two years, whereas approximately 10\% survive for more than a decade \cite{Feldman2022}. This variation makes prognosis difficult at the point of first clinical assessment.

Functional status is commonly assessed with the \gls{ALSFRS-R}, a twelve-item scale covering bulbar, fine-motor, gross-motor, and respiratory function \cite{cedarbaum1999alsfrs}. Each item is scored from 0 to 4, giving a total score between 0 and 48. Repeated assessments allow the rate of functional decline to be estimated, usually as an \gls{ALSFRS-R} slope. This provides a different prognostic view from survival: one describes how quickly daily function is being lost, while the other asks whether a patient survives beyond a defined time horizon. The two outcomes are related, but they are not interchangeable and require different target definitions and evaluation priorities.

Both studies in this dissertation use the Pooled Resource Open-Access ALS Clinical Trials (\gls{PRO-ACT}) database. \gls{PRO-ACT} combines longitudinal records from 23 ALS clinical trials and contains data from approximately 10,000 participants \cite{PROACT,kueffner2019}. Available variables include functional assessments, demographics, vital signs, pulmonary function, laboratory measurements, and treatment records. The database has supported large-scale comparative work, including the ALS progression prediction challenge described by K\"uffner et al.\ \cite{kueffner2019}. Its size makes it useful for model development, but the final cohort depends strongly on the outcome definition, temporal alignment, and missing-data requirements applied in each study.

\section{Motivation}
\label{subsec:motivation}

Prognostic uncertainty affects both ALS research and patient care. Estimates of functional decline can help stratify clinical trial cohorts and identify patients who may require closer follow-up. Survival estimates may also inform the planning of respiratory, nutritional, and palliative support. These predictions should not determine care in isolation, but they may add useful information when interpreted alongside clinical assessment and patient preferences.

The difficulty is not limited to choosing a classifier. Longitudinal clinical data create several opportunities for information leakage, particularly when observations from the same patient are allowed to enter different data partitions. Such overlap can produce optimistic estimates because the model is evaluated on patients it has already encountered during training \cite{Grollemund2019}. Target construction can introduce a similar problem when thresholds are calculated using the full dataset rather than the training partition alone.

Class imbalance also changes how performance should be interpreted. Rapid progressors account for approximately 30\% of the Study~I cohort, whereas Short Survivors represent only 11.6\% in Study~II. A model may therefore obtain a favourable ranking metric while making few useful positive predictions at its chosen decision threshold. Reporting only \gls{ROC-AUC} or \gls{PR-AUC} does not show how many clinically important cases are missed. Recall, precision, specificity, confusion matrices, and the procedure used to select the threshold are needed to describe that behaviour.

Interpretability and reproducibility provide two further constraints. Feature-attribution methods can show whether a model relies on clinically plausible signals, although these explanations describe predictive associations rather than causal effects. Reproducibility requires enough methodological detail to determine whether a published conclusion survives a different data extract, validation design, or modelling environment. Reviews of ALS prognosis research identify recurring limitations in validation, cohort construction, and external evaluation \cite{Papaiz2022,Halbersberg2023}. Papaiz et al.\ \cite{Papaiz2024} offered a suitable case for replication because their BalancedBagging framework addressed a severely imbalanced survival target and included a statistical comparison with single classifiers. Study~II examines which aspects of that result can be reproduced and what changes when the experimental design is extended.

\section{Objectives}
\label{subsec:objectives}

The overall aim of this dissertation is to examine how explainable machine learning behaves across two distinct ALS prognosis tasks when validation, class imbalance, and decision thresholds are treated as part of the modelling problem.

\textbf{Study I: Functional Progression Prediction.} The first study develops a classification pipeline for rapid and slow \gls{ALSFRS-R} progression at three- and six-month horizons, with patients kept separate across data partitions. It investigates the performance achievable from baseline clinical data, the contribution of different feature groups, the effect of class weighting and horizon choice, and whether global and local explanations identify clinically plausible predictive signals. The rapid-progression threshold is estimated within each training fold to avoid using information from held-out patients.

\textbf{Study II: Survival Prognosis Replication and Extension.} The second study partially replicates the BalancedBagging survival classification framework proposed by Papaiz et al.\ \cite{Papaiz2024}. The target is Short Survivor status, defined as death within 24 months of symptom onset. The analysis is extended through Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation, LightGBM, ten individual imbalance-handling strategies, a held-out test evaluation, threshold analysis, uncertainty estimates, and \gls{SHAP}-based interpretation. Its purpose is both to examine the reproducibility of the published BalancedBagging result and to determine whether high \gls{ROC-AUC} corresponds to adequate detection of the minority class.

The studies address different outcomes rather than different versions of the same prediction task. The shared question is whether their conclusions remain credible once patient separation, target construction, class imbalance, decision thresholds, and model explanations are examined explicitly.

\section{Workflow}
\label{subsec:workflow}

The two studies begin from the same 16 \gls{PRO-ACT} source tables but construct separate cohorts. Study~I sets $t_0$ at the first \gls{ALSFRS-R} assessment and uses 35 features measured at or before that date. Study~II follows the representation used by Papaiz et al., deriving 23 ordinal and binary variables that describe demographic characteristics, treatment, respiratory and nutritional status, regional involvement, and functional decline at or near diagnosis. Where a pre-diagnosis functional assessment is unavailable, the nearest recorded visit is used, a limitation considered later in the dissertation.

Each cohort is divided into development and held-out test partitions before model selection. Study~I compares seven classifiers under balanced and unbalanced class-weight settings, with \gls{PR-AUC} used for optimisation. Study~II evaluates seven classifiers across ten resampling strategies and uses \gls{ROC-AUC} to remain aligned with the replication design. Resampling is confined to the training folds. Hyperparameters are selected with Optuna~\cite{optuna2019}, using GroupKFold in Study~I and repeated stratified cross-validation in Study~II. In Study~II, MinMax scaling was fitted before the split; although this did not change the transformed values in the present cohort, it is reported as a preprocessing limitation.

Final evaluation is performed once on the corresponding test partition. Ranking metrics are reported together with threshold-dependent measures and bootstrap confidence intervals. The explanation stage is adapted to each model and outcome: Study~I combines \gls{SHAP}~\cite{lundberg2017shap}, \gls{LIME}~\cite{ribeiro2016lime}, and logistic regression coefficients, whereas Study~II uses TreeSHAP~\cite{lundberg2020treeshap} for LightGBM and KernelSHAP for the multi-layer perceptron.

\section{Document Structure}
\label{subsec:structure}

The remainder of this dissertation is structured as follows.

Chapter~\ref{sec:stateoftheart} reviews ALS prognosis research, with particular attention to functional decline, survival, class imbalance, validation, explainability, and reproducibility.

Chapters~\ref{ch:study1_methods} and~\ref{ch:study1_results} present the methods, results, and discussion for the functional progression study.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} brings the two studies together, discusses their limitations, and identifies directions for further work.
