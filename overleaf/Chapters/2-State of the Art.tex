\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{sec:stateoftheart}

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is increasingly understood as a complex neurodegenerative syndrome rather than a single, homogeneous disease, and this complexity has driven the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) to model prognosis, stratify patients, and support clinical decisions. Recent literature combines large-scale clinical datasets, advanced statistical methods, and deep learning, while more recent work starts to address interpretability and explainable AI (XAI), especially within neurodegenerative disorders.

\section{Clinical and prognostic background of ALS}
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by progressive upper and lower motor neuron loss and a median survival of 3–5 years, with substantial heterogeneity in clinical trajectories~\cite{Feldman2022}. Epidemiologically, incidence increases with age and differs across ancestry groups, reinforcing ALS as a multifactorial syndrome with variable phenotypes and disease courses~\cite{Feldman2022}. Clinical monitoring relies heavily on functional scales such as ALSFRS and ALSFRS-R, widely validated a longitudinal marker of functional decline~\cite{Pancotti2022}. Staging systems, including King’s and MiToS, provide structured clinical milestones associated with survival, though they still fall short of modelling continuous heterogeneity~\cite{Feldman2022}.

Together, these staging systems illustrate the wide variability in disease progression, risk patterns and survival trajectories, which motivates the need for predictive tools capable of capturing clinically meaningful differences across individuals. Figure 1 summarizes these elements.

\section{Machine learning for ALS prognosis: datasets, outcomes and biomarkers}
The application of ML to ALS prognosis has expanded rapidly over the past decade. Two complementary reviews synthesize this literature. Grollemund et al. provides an early, broad overview of ML applications in ALS, covering diagnosis, survival prediction, MRI-based markers and multimodal approaches; they emphasize both the promise of ML and recurring pitfalls such as small sample sizes, biased cohorts and lack of external validation~\cite{Grollemund2019}. Papaiz et al. focus specifically on ML solutions for prognosis, systematically reviewing ten primary studies using clinical, laboratory and imaging variables, and categorizing outcomes into (i) functional disease progression, (ii) survival time, and (iii) need for support (e.g., ventilation, gastrostomy)~\cite{Papaiz2022}.

Across these studies, a relatively consistent set of predictors emerges. ALSFRS / ALSFRS-R scores and their rates of change, disease duration, forced vital capacity (FVC), body mass index (BMI), age at onset and creatinine levels appear repeatedly as key prognostic variables associated with both functional decline and survival~\cite{Papaiz2022}. Papaiz et al. also highlights that most included works rely on standard clinical biomarkers routinely collected in practice, increasing the translational potential of resulting models~\cite{Papaiz2022}.

The heterogeneity of ALSFRS-R decline across patients is particularly relevant for constructing prognostic labels in ML studies. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of 3–12-month ALSFRS-R slopes and the distinction commonly applied between fast and medium–slow progressors.

This distribution highlights both the variability and skewness of functional decline in ALS, reinforcing the need for large datasets and robust preprocessing strategies when building predictive models.

In terms of datasets, the Pooled Resource Open-Access ALS Clinical Trials (PRO-ACT) repository has become the dominant benchmark, with many prognostic models trained on several thousand clinical trial participants~\cite{Pancotti2022}. PRO-ACT offers rich longitudinal ALSFRS(-R), spirometry, vital signs and laboratory data; however, multiple authors stress its limitations: trial inclusion criteria skew the cohort towards younger, less impaired patients, and missing data are substantial, meaning only a fraction of records are usable without imputation~\cite{Papaiz2022,Grollemund2019}.
Several groups therefore complement PRO-ACT with local registry or clinic datasets (e.g., Lisbon or Paris cohorts) to improve external validity and test generalization~\cite{Halbersberg2023}.

Alongside PRO-ACT, national or regional clinical databases and dedicated cohorts (e.g., PULSE, ExonHit) have been used to construct prognostic models in more representative clinical populations, often at the cost of smaller sample sizes~\cite{Halbersberg2023,Papaiz2022}. These datasets typically include demographic variables, site and time of onset, respiratory measures, anthropometrics and ALS-specific variables such as staging or ventilation status, enabling both cross-sectional risk stratification and longitudinal modelling.


\section{Modelling strategies for ALS progression and survival}
\subsection{Classical statistical and probabilistic models}

Traditional approaches to ALS prognosis have relied on Cox proportional hazards models and other regression-based methods, sometimes extended with mixture models or dynamic Bayesian networks (DBNs). Halbersberg and Lerner, reviewed within the AI-and-statistics literature, use DBNs to jointly model ALSFRS and FVC trajectories and dependencies between clinical and respiratory domains, capturing temporal structure in disease progression~\cite{Halbersberg2023}. Probabilistic graphical models have also been applied for survival and functional outcomes, representing ALS progression as a network of conditional dependencies between symptoms, interventions and outcomes.
These methods offer advantages in interpretability and explicit handling of uncertainty but can struggle with high-dimensional feature spaces and complex non-linear patterns in data, especially when interacting biomarkers and multi-system involvement are considered.

\subsection{Supervised ML for functional decline and survival}

A broad range of supervised ML algorithms has been explored to predict ALSFRS(-R) slope, categorize progression speed or estimate short-term survival. Grollemund et al. summarize applications of support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), gradient boosting and neural networks across diagnostic and prognostic tasks, often reporting modest but clinically relevant gains over conventional regression~\cite{Grollemund2019}. Papaiz et al. similarly identify classification and regression schemes predicting survival at fixed time horizons (e.g., 12-month survival), ALSFRS(-R) changes, and need for non-invasive ventilation or gastrostomy~\cite{Papaiz2022}.
The PRO-ACT-based DREAM Phil Bowen ALS Prediction Prize4Life Challenge formalized ALSFRS slope prediction between 3 and 12 months as a benchmark, stimulating numerous ML solutions. Pancotti et al. revisited this challenge with deep learning architectures (fully connected networks, autoencoders and recurrent models) and found that carefully tuned deep models achieved performance comparable to or better than state-of-the-art methods such as Bayesian decision trees and RFs, confirming the feasibility of data-driven ALSFRS slope prediction from early clinical data~\cite{Pancotti2022}.

These results reinforce the increasing role of supervised learning models in ALS prognosis, showing that both gradient-boosting algorithms and deep neural networks can capture clinically meaningful patterns when appropriately optimized.

More recently, Anani et al. combined multiple ALS datasets (PRO-ACT, ExonHit, PULSE) to build models for one-year survival and ALSFRS evolution using logistic/linear regression, k-NN, decision trees, RF and LightGBM. Their key contribution is the rigorous use of metaheuristics—specifically differential evolution—for feature selection, reducing the initial 43 features to 19 while improving balanced accuracy for one-year survival to around 76 percent~\cite{Anani2023}. The authors highlight that reducing dimensionality not only improves accuracy but also enhances interpretability, as the selected features correspond to clinically meaningful variables (e.g., staging, FVC, vital signs).

Complementary work by Qin et al. optimizes deep learning, XGBoost and LightGBM models on PRO-ACT to predict ALS progression and classify onset type (bulbar vs limb). After extensive preprocessing and hyperparameter tuning, a 1D convolutional neural network (CNN) achieved R² values above 0.7 and competitive RMSE for ALSFRS-based progression, while an optimized XGBoost model reached AUC 0.955 and accuracy 0.98 for onset-type classification~\cite{Qin2025}. These results illustrate the power of modern ML and deep learning in capturing complex relationships in multi-dimensional clinical datasets.
Beyond clinical trial data, Imamura et al. propose a different perspective by using deep learning on images of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived motor neurons. A CNN (VGG-16-based) trained to distinguish ALS versus control motor neurons achieved AUC 0.97, demonstrating proof-of-concept for cell-level diagnostic support, even though generalizability and clinical integration remain open issues~\cite{Imamura2022}.

\subsection{Unsupervised and longitudinal pattern discovery}

ALS heterogeneity has also motivated unsupervised approaches to identify progression patterns. Ramamoorthy et al.~\cite{Ramamoorthy2022} apply a mixture of Gaussian processes to sparse longitudinal ALSFRS-R data from multiple cohorts, identifying distinct non-linear progression trajectories associated with meaningful differences in survival. Their work demonstrates that clinically relevant stratification can emerge directly from temporal patterns, even when patient follow-up is irregular or incomplete. Time-series clustering, principal component analysis and UMAP have also been used to reveal subgroups with similar trajectories, often feeding back into supervised models as data-driven stratification. Anani et al. use Kaplan–Meier analysis on model-derived risk scores to derive five survival clusters with distinct profiles, while earlier UMAP-based analyses on the same datasets showed that manifold learning can uncover clinically meaningful subgroups~\cite{Anani2023}. Segura et al.’s NLP study similarly identifies patterns in symptom timelines that differentiate spinal and bulbar ALS, supporting ML-based phenotype clustering from real-world data~\cite{Paganoni2023}.


\section{Explainable and interpretable AI in ALS and neurodegenerative disease}
Although many ALS prognostic models use complex ML techniques, explicit attention to interpretability has historically been limited. Grollemund et al. warned that “black-box” models, small cohorts and lack of transparent validation hinder clinical adoption and trust~\cite{Grollemund2019}. Papaiz et al. further highlight practical issues such as missing data, imbalanced classes and over-reliance on PRO-ACT, which may lead to biased or non-generalizable models unless carefully addressed~\cite{Papaiz2022}.

Several strands of recent work move towards more explainable solutions:

\subsection{Model-level interpretability via feature selection and simpler models}

Anani et al. deliberately combines metaheuristic feature selection with logistic regression, generating models that are both performant and easier to interpret~\cite{Anani2023}. By constraining the feature set to 19 variables and using a linear model, they reduce the “black-box” perception and allow clinicians to relate model outputs to familiar parameters.

\subsection{Post-hoc explainability using XAI frameworks}

Qin et al. explicitly adopt SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) to provide local and global explanations for their optimized XGBoost model. SHAP identifies ALSFRS-R subscores, time since diagnosis and laboratory parameters as key drivers of individual risk predictions, aligning model reasoning with clinical understanding~\cite{Qin2025}.

\section{Limitations of current approaches and open gaps}
Across the reviewed literature, several consistent limitations and open challenges emerge:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dataset bias and generalizability.} Heavy reliance on PRO-ACT and similar clinical-trial datasets risks models that perform well on selected trial populations but generalize poorly to real-world clinical cohorts, which tend to be older, more impaired at baseline and more heterogeneous~\cite{Papaiz2022,Grollemund2019}. Some studies mitigate this by performing external validation on independent registries, but this remains the exception rather than the standard practice~\cite{Halbersberg2023}.

    \item \textbf{Handling of missingness and temporal complexity.} Missing data is pervasive, especially over long follow-up periods. Simple imputation strategies---such as mean imputation or last observation carried forward---remain common despite their limitations~\cite{Papaiz2022}. Temporal structure is frequently collapsed into summary statistics (e.g., early slopes), rather than modelled using dedicated longitudinal architectures. Although DBNs and recurrent deep learning models begin to address this, their use in ALS remains sporadic~\cite{Halbersberg2023,Pancotti2022}.

    \item \textbf{Outcome definitions and evaluation.} Studies use diverse outcome definitions (survival thresholds, slopes, trajectory classes), which complicates benchmarking. Evaluation protocols vary widely, and some works risk optimistic estimates due to data leakage or non-patient-independent validation. Standardized, clinically aligned outcome definitions and robust validation remain essential.

    \item \textbf{Limited integration of multimodal data.} Many studies focus on clinical and demographic covariates, while fewer integrate multimodal signals (imaging, electrophysiology, digital biomarkers). Where multimodal fusion is attempted, it often suffers from limited sample size and inconsistent data availability across modalities.

    \item \textbf{Insufficiently developed explainability and user-centered design.} Although SHAP and other XAI tools are increasingly applied, many explanations remain post-hoc and are not designed with clinical workflows in mind. More user-centered evaluation is needed to ensure that explanations are actionable and trusted.

    \item \textbf{Focus on short-term predictions and group-level performance.} A large fraction of the literature emphasizes short-term prediction horizons or group-level performance metrics, with fewer studies focusing on individual-level decision thresholds, calibration, and clinical deployment constraints.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Synthesis and implications for future work}
Overall, the state of the art in ALS prognosis has progressed from simple regression models on small cohorts to sophisticated ML and deep learning approaches trained on large clinical-trial datasets and real-world registries. Reviews by Grollemund et al. and Papaiz et al. document major advances---particularly in survival prediction and functional-decline modelling---while also repeatedly emphasizing issues of dataset bias, missingness and validation quality~\cite{Grollemund2019,Papaiz2022}. Methodologically, recent studies demonstrate that combining rigorous preprocessing, feature selection and advanced ML learners (e.g., deep neural networks, gradient boosting) can yield accurate models for ALSFRS slope, short-term survival, and phenotype classification~\cite{Pancotti2022,Anani2023,Qin2025}. At the same time, explainability and clinical usability remain underdeveloped. Feature-selection pipelines using metaheuristics or interpretable models~\cite{Anani2023} provide one route, while SHAP-based analysis and broader XAI frameworks offer post-hoc interpretability for complex models~\cite{Qin2025}. The wider XAI-in-NDDs literature highlights the importance of integrating interpretability, multimodal fusion, ethical standards and user-centered design into future diagnostic and prognostic systems~\cite{Wang2025}.

Within this landscape, there is significant opportunity for research that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Builds prognostic models on clinically representative cohorts, reducing bias and improving generalizability.
    \item Balances predictive performance with interpretability, for example through hybrid ML/XAI approaches.
    \item Focuses on clinically actionable outcomes, such as progression phenotypes, risk-group stratification, and decision-support thresholds aligned with care pathways.
    \item Employs robust internal and external validation, aligning modelling choices with clinical workflows and intended use.
\end{enumerate}

Such contributions would directly address the limitations highlighted across current ALS and XAI research and support more personalized care, improved clinical decision-support systems and enhanced trial design for this highly heterogeneous disease.
